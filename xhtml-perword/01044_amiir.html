अमीर (amiir)

adjective/noun Mainly an adjective meaning 'rich, wealthy'; also used as a noun for a wealthy person, and historically as a title for a Muslim noble or chieftain.
Sense 1 : rich, wealthy
adjective
Synonyms: धनी (dhanii), दौलतमंद (daulatmand), संपन्न (sampann, formal)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is the everyday word Delhi-ites reach for when gossiping about someone's money -- अमीर लोग (amiir log, 'rich people') is a very common phrase. It pairs naturally in the classic contrast अमीर और गरीब (amiir aur gariib, 'rich and poor'), a phrase you'll hear constantly in news, songs, and everyday moralizing about inequality.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes confuse अमीर (amiir) with बड़ा (badaa, 'big/great') when trying to say someone is 'well-off' -- अमीर (amiir) specifically points to money/wealth, not size, status, or greatness in general. 2) It describes people (or their lifestyle), not objects being expensive -- for an expensive item, महँगा (mahangaa) is used instead, not अमीर (amiir).

Sense 2 : a rich person, a wealthy man
noun, masculine Used as a noun when referring to 'the rich' or 'a rich man' as a category of person, often in the plural अमीर लोग (amiir log, 'rich people') or contrastively with गरीब (gariib, 'poor person').
Synonyms: धनी व्यक्ति (dhanii vyakti), दौलतमंद (daulatmand)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: You'll often hear this noun use in the same breath as its adjective use, since अमीर (amiir) slides easily between describing a person and naming them as a type -- much like 'rich' can work as both adjective and noun in casual English ('the rich and the poor').

Sense 3 : a Muslim nobleman, chieftain, or high official (historical title)
noun, masculine
Synonyms: सरदार (sardaar, literary), नवाब (navaab)
Frequency: literary
Cultural Note: This older sense comes from Arabic amir, the same root as English 'emir,' and echoes back to Mughal- and Delhi Sultanate-era courts. You'll meet it in history books, period dramas, or Urdu poetry more than in daily conversation.
