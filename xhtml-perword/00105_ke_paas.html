के पास (ke paas)

postposition A compound postposition meaning 'near/at' physically, and, by extension, the standard way to express possession ('have') in Hindi -- both senses are covered below.
Sense 1 : possession (to have)
postposition Used with होना (honaa) in the frame X के पास Y है (X ke paas Y hai, 'X has Y') -- the possessor takes के पास and the thing possessed is the grammatical subject, agreeing with है/हैं/था etc.
Synonyms: के यहाँ (ke yahaan, less common in this sense)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is the classic Hindi way of expressing possession, and it reflects a broader pattern where Hindi prefers 'X is near/with Y' over a direct 'to have' verb -- there's no single verb that means 'to have' the way English does. Think of मेरे पास पैसे हैं (mere paas paise hain, 'I have money') literally as 'near me, money exists' -- once that clicks, the whole construction feels much more natural.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often try to say 'I have' with a direct subject as in English, producing मैं के पास (main ke paas) instead of मेरे पास (mere paas) -- पास (paas) always attaches to the oblique/possessive form of the pronoun, not the plain subject pronoun. 2) The verb agrees with the thing possessed, not with the possessor -- मेरे पास किताबें हैं (mere paas kitaaben hain, 'I have books') takes हैं (hain) because किताबें (kitaaben) is plural, not मैं (main). 3) के पास (ke paas) is used for things you can physically hold or carry (money, books, a car); for relationships, qualities, or abstract things like time or illness, के (ke) alone or other constructions are often more natural -- learners overextend के पास (ke paas) to every 'have' sentence.
Sample Sentences:
मेरे पास पैसे हैं। I have money.
(mere paas paise hain।)

Sense 2 : physical proximity (near, close to)
postposition Takes the oblique form of whatever precedes it, as with all compound postpositions (e.g. स्टेशन के पास (steshan ke paas, 'near the station')).
Synonyms: के नज़दीक (ke nazdiik), के करीब (ke kariib)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Very handy for giving directions in a city like Delhi, where landmarks matter more than street addresses -- e.g. मंदिर के पास (mandir ke paas, 'near the temple') is often a more useful direction than any formal address.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes confuse के पास (ke paas) with के आस-पास (ke aas-paas), which means 'around/in the vicinity of' (a wider, less precise area) rather than 'right next to'.
Sample Sentences:
मेरा घर मंदिर के पास है। My house is near the temple.
(meraa ghar mandir ke paas hai।)
