दुख (dukh)

noun/verb Mainly a noun meaning sorrow, pain, or suffering; also used as a verb meaning to hurt or ache. Also spelled दुःख (duhkh) with the visarga -- both forms are common.
Sense 1 : sorrow, grief, sadness
noun, masculine
Synonyms: ग़म (gham), शोक (shok, more formal/literary), तकलीफ़ (takliif, often overlaps with hardship/trouble)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: दुख (dukh) is one half of the classic pair सुख-दुख (sukh-dukh, 'joys and sorrows'), which comes up constantly in everyday reflection on life -- as in wishing someone well 'through सुख-दुख (sukh-dukh)'. It carries a gentler, more resigned emotional weight than English 'grief', closer to the quiet ache of disappointment or loss.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often use दर्द (dard) and दुख (dukh) interchangeably, but दर्द (dard) leans toward physical pain while दुख (dukh) is the word for emotional grief or sorrow. 2) The common phrase दुख होना (dukh honaa) takes a को-marked experiencer (मुझे दुख हुआ (mujhe dukh huaa, 'I felt sad')), not ने (ne) -- learners sometimes reach for ने (ne) here out of habit.
Sample Sentences:
मुझे उसकी बात सुनकर बहुत दुख हुआ। I felt very sad when I heard what he said.
(mujhe uskii baat sunkar bahut dukh huaa।)

Sense 2 : pain, hardship, trouble (physical or circumstantial)
noun, masculine
Synonyms: कष्ट (kasht), तकलीफ़ (takliif), परेशानी (pareshaanii)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: In phrases like दुख उठाना (dukh uthaanaa, 'to bear hardship/suffering') or दुख सहना (dukh sahnaa, 'to endure suffering'), दुख (dukh) shifts from pure emotion toward lived hardship -- the struggles and troubles of life itself, not just a feeling about them.

Sense 3 : to ache, to hurt (of a body part)
verb, intransitive Used intransitively with a body part as subject, e.g. सिर दुखना (sir dukhnaa, 'for the head to ache'); the person experiencing it is marked with का (kaa) or के (ke) possessive on the body part, not as a को-subject.
Synonyms: दर्द करना (dard karnaa, more common colloquial phrase for the same idea)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This verb shows Hindi's classic body-as-agent pattern: the body part itself does the aching, and you are just the possessor standing by. It is less common in casual speech than the phrase दर्द होना (dard honaa) or दर्द करना (dard karnaa), but still heard, especially in slightly more literary or careful speech.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes try to make the person the grammatical subject (as in English 'my head hurts me'), but Hindi puts the body part itself as the subject: मेरा सिर दुखता है (meraa sir dukhtaa hai, 'my head aches'), literally 'my head aches.'
Sample Sentences:
मेरा सिर दुख रहा है। My head is aching.
(meraa sir dukh rahaa hai।)
