मन (man)

noun Mainly a noun meaning 'mind/heart' as the seat of feeling, will, and mood, and shows up constantly in fixed phrases like मन करना (man karnaa) and मन लगना (man lagnaa); a separate, unrelated sense is a traditional unit of weight.
Sense 1 : mind, heart, inner feeling/mood
noun, masculine Frequently used with को (ko) or as the subject of दative-subject constructions, e.g. मन करना (man karnaa, 'to feel like doing something') and मन लगना (man lagnaa, 'to feel absorbed/interested').
Synonyms: दिल (dil), चित्त (chitt, literary), जी (jii)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: मन (man) is one of those wonderfully untranslatable Hindi words -- it is not quite 'heart', not quite 'mind', but the inner emotional-volitional self that has its own moods and desires almost independently of you. You'll hear Delhi aunties say मन नहीं है (man nahiin hai, 'I don't feel like it') the way English speakers might say 'I'm just not in the mood', treating मन (man) as a slightly separate entity inside you that has to agree before you do something.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often translate मन (man) as 'mind' in the purely intellectual sense (like English 'brain'), but it usually points to feelings, mood, or inclination rather than logical thought -- दिमाग (dimaagh) is closer to 'brain/intellect'. 2) The common phrase मेरा मन नहीं लग रहा (meraa man nahiin lag rahaa, 'I don't feel like it/I'm restless') can confuse learners who expect a subject-verb structure rather than this dative-subject feel. 3) मन करना (man karnaa) takes an oblique infinitive + का (kaa), as in खाने का मन कर रहा है (khaane kaa man kar rahaa hai, 'I feel like eating'), which learners sometimes try to build with को (ko) instead.

(aaj meraa man nahiin hai baahar jaane kaa।)

Sense 2 : traditional unit of weight (roughly 37 kg, varies by region/era)
noun, masculine
Frequency: low
Cultural Note: This मन (man) is the old bazaar unit of weight -- the 'maund' of colonial-era trade records -- historically used for grain, cotton, and other bulk goods; it survives today mostly in older literature, proverbs, and rural markets rather than everyday city speech.
