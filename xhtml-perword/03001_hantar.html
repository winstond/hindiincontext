हंटर (hantar)

noun A loanword from English 'hunter,' used mainly for a whip/lash and, secondarily, for a strict disciplinarian person; also survives as a surname/nickname element.
Sense 1 : whip, lash (especially a leather riding-crop/whip used for discipline)
noun, masculine
Synonyms: कोड़ा (kodaa)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This is a classic colonial-era English loanword: British officers and schoolmasters used actual leather 'hunting whips' for discipline, and the Hindi word settled onto that specific object. Older schoolteachers in Delhi-NCR households are sometimes jokingly remembered as keeping a हंटर (hantar) handy, which feeds directly into the word's figurative sense of a strict disciplinarian.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes assume हंटर (hantar) only means a person who hunts, carrying over the English sense of 'hunter' -- in everyday Hindi this word almost always refers to the whip/lash object, not to a person who hunts animals. 2) The 'person who hunts' sense is not native to this word; for that meaning speakers say शिकारी (shikaarii).

Sense 2 : a strict, harsh disciplinarian (figurative use of 'whip')
noun, masculine Used as an epithet for a person, often preceded by a possessive or in apposition, e.g. calling a strict boss or teacher हंटर (hantar).
Synonyms: सख्त अफसर (sakht aphsar, strict officer/boss)
Frequency: low
Cultural Note: This sense grows directly out of the whip meaning: a person who 'wields the हंटर (hantar)' becomes, by extension, someone feared for their strictness -- much like calling a harsh boss a 'slave-driver' in English.
Common Mistakes: 1) This figurative use is informal and somewhat dated -- it is heard more from older speakers describing a stern teacher, officer, or boss than in everyday youth slang, so learners should not overuse it in casual conversation expecting it to sound current.
