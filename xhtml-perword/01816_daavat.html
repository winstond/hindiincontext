दावत (daavat)

noun A warm, social word meaning a feast or invitation to eat; also used in an extended idiomatic sense of 'inviting trouble.'
Sense 1 : feast, banquet, party involving food
noun, feminine
Synonyms: ज़ियाफ़त (ziyaafat, formal), भोज (bhoj, formal)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: In Delhi-NCR households, दावत (daavat) is the word you hear when someone announces a big family meal, a wedding-related feast, or even just 'come over, I am cooking something special.' It carries an emotional warmth of hospitality, not just an event on a calendar.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often reach for 'party' as a direct translation, but दावत (daavat) centers specifically on food being served, not the broader English sense of a social party (which is often just पार्टी (paartii)). 2) The verb pairing to know is देना (denaa) for hosting ('दावत देना' daavat denaa, 'to host a feast/invite for a meal') and खाना (khaanaa) for attending ('दावत खाना' daavat khaanaa, 'to eat at a feast').

Sense 2 : invitation (to a meal or gathering)
noun, feminine Commonly used with देना (denaa, 'to give/extend') and मिलना (milnaa, 'to receive') to describe extending or getting an invitation.
Synonyms: न्यौता (nyautaa, colloquial), निमंत्रण (nimantran, formal)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This sense overlaps closely with the 'feast' sense above -- the invitation and the meal it leads to are seen as one continuous social event in everyday Hindi thought, unlike in English where 'invitation' and 'feast' feel more separate.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes use दावत (daavat) for any kind of formal invitation (like a wedding card), but that is more naturally निमंत्रण (nimantran) or written as शादी का कार्ड (shaadii kaa kaard, 'wedding card'); दावत (daavat) specifically implies food is involved.

Sense 3 : idiomatic: an invitation to trouble or punishment
noun, feminine Appears in the fixed idiomatic phrase मार की दावत देना (maar kii daavat denaa, 'to invite a beating/trouble') and similar constructions where दावत (daavat) is paired with something undesirable.
Frequency: low
Cultural Note: This is a playful, ironic twist on the warm hospitality sense: instead of inviting someone to eat, you are 'inviting' trouble or a scolding onto yourself, e.g. जब बच्चा शरारत करता है तो मार की दावत देता है (jab bachchaa sharaarat kartaa hai to maar kii daavat detaa hai, 'when the child misbehaves, he invites a beating'). The humor lies in reusing a hospitality word for something unpleasant.
Common Mistakes: 1) This figurative use only works in set phrases like मार की दावत (maar kii daavat); you cannot freely substitute दावत (daavat) for 'invitation' in every negative context -- it needs the idiomatic frame to land correctly.
Sample Sentences:
