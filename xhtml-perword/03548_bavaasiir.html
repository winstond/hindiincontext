बवासीर (bavaasiir)

noun A medical/colloquial term for hemorrhoids/piles, also used figuratively for a chronic nuisance or headache-causing problem.
Sense 1 : hemorrhoids, piles (medical condition)
noun, feminine
Synonyms: अर्श (arsh, formal/medical), पाइल्स (paails, English loanword, common in clinics)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This word comes up often in TV and roadside ads for herbal remedies (हकीम (hakiim) and देसी दवा (desii davaa) shops), since piles are a common ailment linked to spicy food and sedentary lifestyles in everyday conversation. It is spoken about more openly than many other health issues, often with a mix of embarrassment and dark humor.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes treat this as a rude or taboo word to avoid entirely -- it is a normal, if slightly embarrassing, medical term, similar to how 'hemorrhoids' functions in English; people do talk about it with doctors and family without much hesitation. 2) Note the gender is feminine despite ending in a consonant sound -- it takes feminine agreement, e.g. बवासीर हो गई (bavaasiir ho gaii, 'piles occurred/developed'), not हो गया.

(mujhe bavaasiir kii shikaayat ho gaii hai।)

Sense 2 : figurative: a persistent, irritating problem or headache (person or situation)
noun, feminine Used in phrases like बवासीर बन गया है (bavaasiir ban gayaa hai, 'has become a real pain') to describe a chronically annoying person or issue.
Synonyms: सिरदर्द (sirdard, 'headache', more polite equivalent), झंझट (jhanjhat, hassle, milder)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: Much like English speakers might jokingly call a nagging problem 'a pain,' Hindi speakers reach for बवासीर (bavaasiir) for comic exaggeration -- calling an annoying relative or a repeated bureaucratic hassle a बवासीर (bavaasiir) is meant to sound funny and a bit crude, not medically literal.
Common Mistakes: 1) This figurative use is casual/crude in tone -- appropriate among close friends venting about an annoying boss or problem, but not in polite or formal company. 2) Learners may not realize this figurative sense is quite common in spoken banter, more so than its literal medical use in daily small talk.
