बढ़कर (badhkar)

adverb/verb The conjunctive-participle form of बढ़ना (badhnaa, 'to increase/advance'), used on its own as an adverb/adjective-like word meaning 'exceeding' or 'more than', especially in the fixed phrase से बढ़कर (se badhkar, 'more than, superior to').
Sense 1 : exceeding, more than (in comparisons)
adverb Occurs almost always after से (se) attached to the thing being surpassed: X से बढ़कर Y (X se badhkar Y, 'Y is greater/better than X').
Synonyms: ज़्यादा (zyaadaa), अधिक (adhik, formal)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: You'll hear सबसे बढ़कर (sabse badhkar, 'most importantly, above all') constantly in spoken Hindi when someone is about to name the single most important point, much like English speakers say 'above all' or 'most importantly' -- it's a great phrase to have ready-made in your active vocabulary.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often try to build comparatives here the way they would with ज़्यादा (zyaadaa) alone, forgetting that बढ़कर (badhkar) needs से (se) on the noun being compared -- सबसे बढ़कर (sabse badhkar, 'above all, most of all') is the most common fixed pattern and is worth memorizing as a chunk. 2) Because it looks like a verb form, learners sometimes try to conjugate it for tense or agreement, but in this adverbial use it stays frozen regardless of subject.
Sample Sentences:
सबसे बढ़कर, उसने हमेशा सच बोला। Most importantly, he always told the truth.
(sabse badhkar, usne hameshaa sach bolaa।)

Sense 2 : having increased, having advanced (conjunctive participle)
verb, intransitive This is the plain conjunctive-participle use of बढ़ना (badhnaa) -- 'having increased/advanced' -- linking two clauses, e.g. दाम बढ़कर दोगुने हो गए (daam badhkar dogune ho gae, 'the prices increased and doubled'). It shares its form with the adverbial sense above but functions as an ordinary verbal participle, not a fixed comparative marker.
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This use is just the ordinary participle of बढ़ना (badhnaa) doing normal verb work -- worth noticing that the same shape बढ़कर (badhkar) does double duty as both a literal 'having increased' and the fixed comparative adverb above, so context tells you which job it's doing.
