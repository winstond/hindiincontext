साहब (saahab)

noun A respectful term of address/reference used for men, ranging from 'sir' to 'gentleman' to 'husband' in certain family registers; also survives as a suffix of respect attached to names or titles.
Sense 1 : sir / mister (respectful address)
noun, masculine Used both as a direct vocative ('साहब, ज़रा सुनिए' (saahab, zaraa sunie, 'Sir, listen please')) and attached after a name or title as an honorific suffix, e.g. शर्मा साहब (sharmaa saahab, 'Mr. Sharma').
Synonyms: श्रीमान (shriimaan, formal), जनाब (janaab, formal/Urdu register)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: You will hear this constantly from shopkeepers, drivers, and household help addressing customers or employers -- 'साहब, कहाँ जाना है?' (saahab, kahaan jaanaa hai?, 'Sir, where to?') from an auto-wallah is everyday Delhi soundscape. It carries a faint colonial-era echo (from Persian/Arabic via Mughal and then British administrative usage), which is why it still shows up in domestic-service and hierarchical contexts more than among equals.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often use साहब (saahab) for any man out of general politeness, but native speakers reserve it for someone of perceived higher status, authority, or a customer/service relationship -- overusing it toward peers can sound oddly formal or even sarcastic. 2) When suffixed to a name, it comes after the name/title (शर्मा साहब (sharmaa saahab)), never before, unlike 'Mr.' in English.

Sense 2 : husband (colloquial, often said by or about a wife)
noun, masculine Typically used in third person when a wife refers to her husband, e.g. साहब घर पर नहीं हैं (saahab ghar par nahiin hain, 'My husband isn't home'), often within domestic or household-staff speech.
Synonyms: पति (pati), शौहर (shauhar, colloquial/Urdu register)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This usage is closely tied to household hierarchies -- a maid or cook might say साहब आ गए (saahab aa gae, 'the sir/husband has arrived') meaning the master of the house, blending the 'boss' and 'husband' senses since traditionally he was both in that domestic context.
Common Mistakes: 1) This sense is mostly heard in reported/indirect speech about a husband, not as a direct term of address to him -- learners sometimes try to call their own husband साहब (saahab) directly, which sounds off; it is more commonly used by domestic help referring to the man of the house, or semi-jokingly by a wife.
