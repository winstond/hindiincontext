चटनी (chatnii)

noun A single, concrete sense: the tangy/spicy accompaniment made of ground fruits, herbs, or spices, plus a common figurative extension for 'making mincemeat' of something.
Sense 1 : chutney (condiment)
noun, feminine
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: No North Indian meal feels complete without at least one चटनी (chatnii) on the plate -- green (mint-coriander) with snacks, tamarind with samosas, coconut with South Indian food. Every household has its own recipe passed down through the women of the family, and asking 'इसमें चटनी क्या है' (ismen chatnii kyaa hai, 'what's in this chutney') is a classic way to start a kitchen conversation.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often assume चटनी (chatnii) means the sweet bottled 'chutney' sold in Western stores -- in everyday Indian kitchens it usually refers to a fresh, just-ground condiment (mint, coriander, tamarind, coconut, tomato, etc.) made the same day, not a preserved jam. 2) It takes बनाना (banaanaa, 'to make') as its usual verb, not पकाना (pakaanaa, 'to cook') -- most chutneys are ground raw or briefly tempered, so पकाना sounds off to a native ear.
Sample Sentences:

(maan ne aaj pudiine kii chatnii banaaii hai।)

Sense 2 : to make mincemeat of, to smash/pulp (figurative)
noun, feminine Occurs almost exclusively inside the fixed idiom चटनी बनाना (chatnii banaanaa, 'to smash to a pulp') or चटनी बन जाना (chatnii ban jaanaa, 'to get smashed to a pulp'), describing something (or, jokingly/violently, someone) getting completely crushed or destroyed.
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: Very much a spoken, informal image -- Hindi loves food metaphors for violence, so 'उसकी चटनी बन गई' (uskii chatnii ban gaii, 'he got smashed to a pulp') paints a vivid, half-comic picture, often used about a bad accident or a one-sided fight/argument.
Common Mistakes: 1) This figurative use only works in the fixed phrase चटनी बनाना (chatnii banaanaa) or चटनी बन जाना (chatnii ban jaanaa) -- you cannot substitute other chutney-related verbs and keep the 'smashed to bits' meaning.
