गाँववाला (gaanvvaalaa)

noun A compound formed from गाँव (gaanv, 'village') plus the possessive/agentive suffix -वाला (vaalaa), meaning 'a person of the village' -- i.e. a villager, or in another common use, a fellow-villager from one's own native place.
Sense 1 : villager, village-dweller
noun, masculine
Synonyms: देहाती (dehaatii, often has a rustic/unsophisticated connotation), ग्रामीण (graamiin, formal)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: In everyday speech गाँववाला (gaanvvaalaa) is the natural way to say 'villager' -- much more common than the bookish ग्रामीण (graamiin), which you would meet more in a newspaper or government scheme name than in a family conversation.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes treat गाँववाला (gaanvvaalaa) as a fixed word with no feminine form -- it inflects like other -वाला (vaalaa) words: गाँववाली (gaanvvaalii) for a woman, गाँववाले (gaanvvaale) for plural/oblique. 2) It is not automatically pejorative -- context and tone decide whether it means simply 'a village resident' or carries a jab about being unsophisticated.

Sense 2 : someone from one's own village, fellow-villager
noun, masculine This sense is almost always used relationally -- 'मेरे गाँव का' (mere gaanv kaa, 'from my village') is implied even when not stated, so it functions like 'someone from back home' rather than a neutral label for any villager.
Synonyms: हमवतन (hamavtan, broader, means compatriot/fellow countryman)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: In cities like Delhi, migrants often use गाँववाला (gaanvvaalaa) to mean a fellow-migrant from the same native village -- a warm, in-group term, similar to how someone might say 'he's from my hometown' in English. Meeting a गाँववाला (gaanvvaalaa) in a big city is a small, happy occasion worth mentioning.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often miss the relational flavor and translate it flatly as 'villager' when the speaker actually means 'someone from my hometown' -- the difference matters when, say, introducing someone at a gathering in the city.
