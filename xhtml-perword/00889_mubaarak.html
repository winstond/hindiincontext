मुबारक (mubaarak)

adjective/interjection An Urdu/Persian-derived word meaning 'blessed' or 'auspicious,' used almost entirely in fixed congratulatory phrases like नए साल की मुबारक (nae saal kii mubaarak, 'happy new year') and ईद मुबारक (iid mubaarak, 'happy Eid').
Sense 1 : blessed, auspicious, congratulatory
adjective Used almost exclusively in the fixed frame [occasion] + मुबारक (mubaarak) ho, as a congratulatory formula rather than a descriptive adjective in free sentences.
Synonyms: शुभ (shubh), पावन (paavan, literary)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: You will hear this word constantly around festivals: ईद मुबारक (iid mubaarak), होली मुबारक (holii mubaarak), नया साल मुबारक हो (nayaa saal mubaarak ho) -- it works across Hindu, Muslim, and Sikh greetings alike in Delhi-NCR, showing how thoroughly this Persian-origin word has been absorbed into everyday Hindi.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners try to use मुबारक (mubaarak) as a general adjective for 'blessed' in other contexts (like blessing a person or object) -- it is almost always reserved for greetings tied to occasions or events. 2) The verb हो (ho) is often dropped in casual greetings (जन्मदिन मुबारक (janmdin mubaarak, 'happy birthday')) but implied -- learners sometimes wonder where the verb went.

Sense 2 : congratulations!
interjection Functions as a standalone exclamation of congratulations, often paired with a handshake or hug in celebratory moments (weddings, promotions, births).
Synonyms: बधाई हो (badhaaii ho)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Said alone with a big smile, मुबारक! (mubaarak) carries real warmth -- it is the kind of word that gets shouted across a room when good news breaks, especially at weddings and engagements.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes reach for बधाई हो (badhaaii ho) and मुबारक (mubaarak) interchangeably in all contexts, but मुबारक (mubaarak) leans more toward greetings tied to specific named occasions (festivals, new year, marriage) while बधाई हो (badhaaii ho) is more general-purpose for achievements.
