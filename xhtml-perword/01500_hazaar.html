हज़ार (hazaar)

numeral/adjective A single-sense numeral meaning 'thousand', used exactly like an English quantity word but also common in exaggeration and idiom to mean 'countless' or 'many'.
Sense 1 : the number 1,000
numeral
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Delhi shopkeepers and rickshaw-wallahs use हज़ार (hazaar) constantly in haggling over prices -- 'पाँच सौ' (paanch sau, 'five hundred') versus 'एक हज़ार' (ek hazaar, 'one thousand') is the daily rhythm of bargaining. It also appears in the common exaggeration हज़ार बार कहा (hazaar baar kahaa, 'told you a thousand times'), where it just means 'so many times', not a literal count.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often try to inflect हज़ार (hazaar) like an adjective agreeing with the noun it counts -- it stays invariant regardless of the noun's gender or number, e.g. हज़ार किताबें (hazaar kitaaben, 'a thousand books') and हज़ार रुपये (hazaar rupye, 'a thousand rupees') both use the same bare form. 2) In counted amounts, the noun after हज़ार (hazaar) does not take a plural postposition the way English 'thousands of' does -- दो हज़ार लोग (do hazaar log, 'two thousand people') is direct, with no 'of' equivalent inserted.

Sense 2 : countless, innumerable (figurative)
adjective Used before a plural noun to mean 'many' rather than a literal count, as in हज़ार समस्याएँ (hazaar samasyaaen, 'countless problems').
Synonyms: बहुत (bahut), अनेक (anek, more formal)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This figurative sense shows up in everyday complaints and in film dialogue alike -- हज़ार तकलीफ़ें हैं (hazaar takliifen hain, 'there are a thousand troubles') is how someone vents about a difficult situation without meaning a literal 1,000. It grows naturally out of the numeral sense above, the way English speakers also stretch 'a thousand' or 'a million' into hyperbole.
