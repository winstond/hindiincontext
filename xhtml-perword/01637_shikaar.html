शिकार (shikaar)

noun Primarily 'hunt/prey' as a noun, but very frequently used in the figurative sense of becoming a 'victim' of something bad (illness, fraud, circumstance) -- that idiomatic use is often more common in daily speech than the literal hunting sense.
Sense 1 : victim, prey (figurative -- becoming a target of something bad)
noun, masculine Almost always used in the fixed frame X का शिकार होना (kaa shikaar honaa, 'to become a victim/target of X'), where X is the misfortune (बीमारी (biimaarii), धोखे (dhokhe), हादसे (haadse)) in the oblique/genitive case.
Synonyms: पीड़ित (piidit)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is one of those words where Hindi's imagery is worth pausing on: whatever misfortune befalls you (illness, a scam, a car accident) is framed as if you were prey and the misfortune was the hunter. You'll hear this constantly in news reports -- हादसे का शिकार (haadse kaa shikaar, 'victim of an accident') is everyday phrasing, not literary flourish.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often try to translate 'victim' directly with पीड़ित (piidit) in every context, but शिकार होना (shikaar honaa) is the far more natural, conversational choice for 'fell victim to / became a target of' -- e.g. धोखे का शिकार होना (dhokhe kaa shikaar honaa, 'to fall victim to fraud'). 2) The verb paired with this sense is always होना (honaa) (intransitive, 'to become'), not करना (karnaa) -- you don't 'do' शिकार in this sense, you 'become' it.

Sense 2 : hunt, hunting; game/prey (literal)
noun, masculine
Synonyms: आखेट (aakhet, literary)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This is the older, literal sense -- tigers, deer, birds as game -- still alive in phrases like शिकार करना (shikaar karnaa, 'to hunt') and शिकार खेलना (shikaar khelnaa, 'to go hunting'), and in place names and Mughal-era hunting lore (royal शिकारगाह (shikaargaah) hunting grounds). The figurative 'victim' sense above grew directly out of this hunting imagery.

(mughal baadshaah aksar janglon men shikaar khelne jaate the।)
