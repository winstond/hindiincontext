पेच (pech)

noun Primarily a concrete noun for a screw or bolt, with an extended figurative sense meaning a tricky twist, complication, or hidden catch in a situation.
Sense 1 : a tricky twist, complication, or hidden catch
noun, masculine Commonly used in phrases like पेच फँसना (pech phansnaa, 'for a snag/hitch to arise') and पेच निकालना (pech nikaalnaa, 'to find a loophole or clever trick').
Synonyms: उलझन (uljhan), पेचीदगी (pechiidgii)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This is the word you'll hear in office or family discussions when someone says इसमें एक पेच है (ismen ek pech hai, 'there's a catch in this') -- it evokes the image of a screw that won't turn smoothly, extended to any plan that has an unexpected hitch.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often use this only in the singular even when speaking of multiple complications in a plan; native speakers freely say कई पेच (kaii pech, 'several catches') to mean multiple hidden snags. 2) Don't confuse this figurative सेंस with पेचीदा (pechiidaa, the adjective 'complicated') -- पेच (pech) is the noun naming the snag itself.
Sample Sentences:

Sense 2 : screw (the hardware fastener)
noun, masculine
Synonyms: स्क्रू (skroo)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: You'll hear this word at any hardware shop (हार्डवेयर की दुकान, haardveyar kii dukaan) in Delhi when asking for screws to fix furniture or fittings -- the word पेचकस (pechkas, 'screwdriver') is built directly from this noun plus a tool-suffix.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes confuse पेच (pech) with कील (kiil, nail), but a पेच (pech) has threads and is turned in with a screwdriver (पेचकस, pechkas), while a कील (kiil) is hammered in straight.

Sense 3 : a wrestling or kite-flying maneuver/trick
noun, masculine Seen especially in the fixed phrase पेच लड़ाना (pech ladaanaa, 'to lock kite-strings/wrestle skillfully' -- used for both kite-flying duels and wrestling bouts).
Synonyms: दाँव (daanv, wrestling move)
Frequency: low
Cultural Note: During Basant/kite season you'll hear kite-flyers shout पेच लड़ाना (pech ladaanaa) as they try to cut a rival's kite string with a clever maneuver -- the same phrase carries over into kushti (wrestling, kushtii) commentary for a skillful hold or move.
