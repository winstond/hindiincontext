बंदर (bandar)

noun A concrete noun meaning 'monkey'; also used figuratively for a mischievous or fickle person. Also spelled बन्दर (bandar) with the full nasal, an equally valid variant.
Sense 1 : monkey (the animal)
noun, masculine
Synonyms: वानर (vaanar, literary)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Monkeys are a real presence in Delhi-NCR -- around Old Delhi, government buildings, and hill stations, signs warn बंदरों से सावधान (bandron se saavdhaan, 'beware of monkeys'), and people genuinely lock their windows because of them. The word also carries strong religious resonance through Hanuman (hanumaan), who is worshipped as a divine monkey, so बंदर (bandar) is never a purely neutral zoological term the way 'monkey' can feel in English.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes reach for वानर (vaanar) as the default word, but in everyday speech बंदर (bandar) is what people actually say; वानर (vaanar) sounds bookish or mythological, mainly evoking Hanuman and the Ramayana's monkey army.

Sense 2 : a mischievous, fickle, or foolish person (figurative)
noun, masculine
Synonyms: शैतान (shaitaan, mischievous rather than fickle)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: You will hear mothers scold a restless child with अरे बंदर, बैठ जा (are bandar, baith jaa, 'hey monkey, sit down') -- it is warm, not harsh. The idiom बंदर क्या जाने अदरक का स्वाद (bandar kyaa jaane adrak kaa svaad, 'what does a monkey know of the taste of ginger') is a classic way of saying someone lacks the refinement to appreciate something good.
Common Mistakes: 1) Calling a child बंदर (bandar) is affectionate teasing, not an insult -- learners sometimes hesitate to use it thinking it is rude, but parents and grandparents say it constantly to naughty kids.
