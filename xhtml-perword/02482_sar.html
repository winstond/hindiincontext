सर (sar)

noun Two main threads: the everyday variant of सिर (sir, 'head'), and the borrowed respectful address term 'sir' used for teachers, bosses, and officials.
Sense 1 : head (body part)
noun, masculine
Synonyms: सिर (sir)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: In everyday Delhi speech, सर (sar) is what you will actually hear when someone says 'my head hurts' -- मेरे सर में दर्द है (mere sar men dard hai, 'my head hurts') -- while सिर (sir) leans slightly more formal or literary. Think of them as a spoken/written pair rather than two separate words.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often treat सर (sar) and सिर (sir) as different words with different meanings, when they refer to the same body part -- सर (sar) is simply the more casual, commonly-heard spoken form. 2) In fixed idioms and more formal or written contexts, सिर (sir) is the form usually seen -- e.g. सिर दर्द (sir dard, 'headache') is more standard in writing than सर दर्द (sar dard), even though the latter is heard in speech.

Sense 2 : sir (respectful address, from English)
noun, masculine Used as a direct term of address or attached after a name/title, e.g. शर्मा सर (sharmaa sar, 'Mr. Sharma, sir') -- functions like a vocative, not a descriptive noun.
Synonyms: साहब (saahab)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Walk into almost any Indian classroom or office and you will hear students and juniors address men in authority as सर (sar) -- it has become fully naturalized in Hindi, sitting alongside साहब (saahab) as a marker of respect, especially in urban, English-influenced settings like Delhi-NCR.
Common Mistakes: 1) This सर (sar) is a straight borrowing from English 'sir' and is used constantly for teachers and bosses in India, but it does not inflect or take postpositions the way the native word सर (sar, 'head') does -- learners sometimes mistakenly try to put it in oblique case or pluralize it as if it were a regular Hindi noun.
