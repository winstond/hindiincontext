नमकीन (namkiin)

noun/adjective Basically means 'salty' -- as an adjective it describes taste, and as a noun it refers to the whole category of savory, salty snacks (namkiin) you'd serve with tea or during festivals.
Sense 1 : savory snacks (collective term)
noun, feminine Used as a mass/collective noun -- you don't usually count individual items with it the way you'd count पकौड़े (pakaude); it refers to the category as a whole, similar to how 'snacks' works in English.
Synonyms: नमकीन चीज़ें (namkiin chiizen, descriptive phrase, not a true synonym)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Walk into any Indian grocery or sweet shop and you'll see a glass case of नमकीन (namkiin) sold by weight alongside मिठाई (mithaaii, sweets) -- the two categories are the standard pairing for guests, festivals, and tea-time. Families often keep a steel container of नमकीन (namkiin) at home specifically for unexpected visitors.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes treat नमकीन (namkiin) as always referring to one specific item, when it actually covers a whole family of fried, spiced, salty snacks -- sev, chanachur, mixture, and so on -- sold loose by weight in shops. 2) Don't confuse it with नाश्ता (naashtaa) 'breakfast/snack time' -- नमकीन (namkiin) is the food itself, not the occasion of eating.

Sense 2 : salty (taste)
adjective
Synonyms: नमकीला (namkiilaa, less common variant)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is the word you'll hear when someone complains कि सब्ज़ी बहुत नमकीन है (sabzii bahut namkiin hai, 'the vegetable dish is too salty') -- it's the everyday opposite of मीठा (miithaa, sweet) when describing food.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes reach for नमक वाला (namak vaalaa) to mean 'salty,' but नमकीन (namkiin) is the natural adjective a native speaker uses for taste, as opposed to describing something as literally 'containing salt.'
