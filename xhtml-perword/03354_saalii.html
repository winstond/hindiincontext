साली (saalii)

noun Primarily a kinship term for a wife's sister; also used, often crudely, as a feminine intensifying abuse/expletive.
Sense 1 : wife's sister (sister-in-law)
noun, feminine
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: In North Indian families the साली-जीजा (saalii-jiijaa) relationship is famously teasing and jokey -- there's a whole genre of banter, songs, and jokes built around a man flirting playfully with his साली (saalii), since the relationship is culturally coded as light and informal compared to stricter in-law relationships. This is also the root of the term's slide into an insult (see the other sense): the playful familiarity of the word got weaponized into casual abuse.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often use साली (saalii) generically for 'sister-in-law' the way English does, but Hindi kinship terms are relationship-specific: साली (saalii) is only the wife's sister, while the husband's sister is नंद (nand) and the brother's wife is भाभी (bhaabhii). 2) Because of its very common insult sense (see the other entry), saying साली (saalii) casually in front of strangers to mean 'sister-in-law' can momentarily sound odd out of context -- tone and surrounding words make the meaning clear to native speakers.

Sense 2 : vulgar term of abuse (feminine, mild-to-crude expletive)
noun, feminine Used as a standalone expletive or as an intensifying prefix before a noun, similar to how साला (saalaa) is used for men -- e.g. साली किस्मत (saalii kismat, 'damn luck').
Synonyms: कुतिया (kutiyaa, much cruder), नालायक (naalaayak, milder alternative insult)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This is the feminine counterpart to the extremely common male filler-abuse साला (saalaa); both literally mean an in-law term but have drifted into casual cursing, heard constantly in Delhi street talk, Bollywood dialogue, and frustrated exclamations. It's the kind of word a learner will hear far more than they should ever say themselves.
Common Mistakes: 1) This sense is street-register and can be genuinely offensive depending on tone and target -- it is not interchangeable with the polite kinship sense just because the spelling is identical; context alone tells native speakers which one is meant. 2) Learners should be very cautious using this themselves, since even 'mild' filler use among close friends can land as rude from an outsider or non-native speaker.
