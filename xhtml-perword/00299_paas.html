पास (paas)

postposition/noun Mainly a postposition of possession/proximity used with के (ke) ('near, in the possession of'); also a borrowed noun meaning 'pass' (bus pass, exam pass). The possession sense पास (paas) है (hai) is one of the first dative-style constructions learners meet.
Sense 1 : in someone's possession, have
postposition Used in the compound postposition के पास (ke paas) following an oblique noun/pronoun, with होना (honaa) to express possession: X के पास Y है (X ke paas Y hai, 'X has Y'). The possessor is marked with के पास, not से or को.
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is the classic 'have' construction in Hindi, and it trips up almost every English speaker at first because there is no verb that means 'to have' the way English uses it. Instead Hindi treats possession as proximity: the thing is metaphorically 'near' you, so मेरे पास किताब है (mere paas kitaab hai, 'I have a book') literally reads as 'near me a book is'. Once this clicks, it unlocks a whole family of similar dative-flavored constructions.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often try को (ko) or से (se) instead of के पास (ke paas) when expressing possession, carrying over patterns from लगना (lagnaa) or चाहिए (chaahie) constructions. 2) मेरे पास (mere paas) is used for things you are currently carrying or own, not for relationships or abstract qualities (for those, में (men) or का/की/के (kaa/kii/ke) is often better) -- so मेरे पास एक भाई है (mere paas ek bhaaii hai) sounds odd to a native ear for 'I have a brother', where मेरा एक भाई है (meraa ek bhaaii hai) is natural.
Sample Sentences:
मेरे पास एक किताब है। I have a book.
(mere paas ek kitaab hai।)

Sense 2 : physical nearness, close to
postposition Also used in के पास (ke paas) form to mark spatial proximity to a place or person, distinct from the possession sense: घर के पास (ghar ke paas, 'near the house').
Synonyms: नज़दीक (nazdiik), करीब (kariib)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: In Delhi, you will constantly hear directions given with के पास (ke paas) anchored to a landmark rather than an address -- मेट्रो स्टेशन के पास (metro steshan ke paas, 'near the metro station') is far more useful to an auto driver than a street number.

Sense 3 : an official pass (bus pass, entry pass, exam pass)
noun, masculine A borrowed noun from English 'pass'; commonly takes light verbs like मिलना (milnaa, 'to be obtained') or बनवाना (banvaanaa, 'to get made') rather than being used with के पास (ke paas).
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: Every Delhi college student knows the ritual of getting a बस पास (bas paas, 'bus pass') made at the start of term, and 'पास होना' (paas honaa) doubles as the everyday way to say you passed an exam -- रोहन पास हो गया (rohan paas ho gayaa, 'Rohan passed').
Common Mistakes: 1) This noun sense is unrelated to the postposition sense despite identical spelling and pronunciation -- learners sometimes assume a semantic link where there is none beyond coincidence of borrowing.
Sample Sentences:
