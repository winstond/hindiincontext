न जाने (n jaane)

adverb A fixed conversational phrase meaning 'who knows' or 'goodness knows' -- literally 'let it not be known,' from जानना (jaannaa, 'to know') in a negated subjunctive form. It has essentially one core use: expressing uncertainty or bewilderment about something.
Sense 1 : who knows / goodness knows (expressing uncertainty)
adverb Sits at the start of a clause and colors the whole statement with uncertainty, much like English 'goodness knows why...' or 'who can say...'. Commonly followed by a question word: न जाने क्यों (n jaane kyon, 'who knows why'), न जाने कब (n jaane kab, 'who knows when'), न जाने कहाँ (n jaane kahaan, 'who knows where').
Synonyms: पता नहीं (pataa nahiin), भगवान जाने (bhagvaan jaane, adds a devotional flavor), कौन जाने (kaun jaane)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is one of those wonderfully expressive Hindi phrases that native speakers reach for constantly to shrug off a question they can't answer -- said with a little tilt of the head and raised eyebrows, it does a lot of emotional work in very few words. You'll hear it everywhere from a mother wondering न जाने बच्चा कहाँ चला गया (n jaane bachchaa kahaan chalaa gayaa, 'who knows where the child has gone') to more philosophical musings about fate and unpredictability.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners try to conjugate जाने (jaane) here as if it were a regular verb form agreeing with a subject -- it doesn't; the whole phrase is frozen and invariant regardless of person, gender, or number. 2) Don't confuse this with the literal negative imperative/subjunctive 'may [someone] not know' -- in real speech न जाने (n jaane) is almost always this fixed idiom of bewilderment, not a live command about someone's knowledge.
