सुबह (subah)

noun/adverb A single core meaning -- 'morning' -- functioning as both a noun and a time adverb ('in the morning'); the two uses share the same word without any change in form.
Sense 1 : morning (the time of day)
noun, feminine
Synonyms: प्रातः (praatah, literary), भोर (bhor, early dawn)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: सुबह (subah) is one of the first time-words every learner picks up, right alongside शाम (shaam, evening) and रात (raat, night). In Delhi households you will constantly hear सुबह की चाय (subah kii chaay, 'morning tea') as its own little ritual, almost a fixed phrase -- the morning cup that starts the day before anything else happens.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often add a postposition where none is needed: सुबह को (subah ko) is heard but plain सुबह (subah) alone already means 'in the morning' when used adverbially, so की (kii)/को (ko) is optional and often dropped in casual speech. 2) Don't confuse with सवेरा (saveraa), which is a near-synonym but leans slightly more toward 'the early part of the day' as an event/moment rather than the general noun.
Sample Sentences:
मुझे सुबह जल्दी उठना पसंद है। I like getting up early in the morning.
(mujhe subah jaldii uthnaa pasand hai।)

Sense 2 : in the morning
adverb Used adverbially without any postposition: सुबह मिलते हैं (subah milte hain, 'let's meet in the morning') -- the bare noun form does the adverbial job directly.
Synonyms: सवेरे (savere), प्रातः (praatah, literary)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This adverbial use is everywhere in daily planning talk: सुबह जल्दी उठना (subah jaldii uthnaa, 'to get up early in the morning') is practically a national conversation topic, especially among parents urging kids (or themselves) to be more disciplined.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes insert में (men) as if 'in the morning' needs a locative marker the way English does, producing सुबह में (subah men); this is understood but sounds slightly off compared to the natural bare सुबह (subah).
