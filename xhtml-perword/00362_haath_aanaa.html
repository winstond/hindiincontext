हाथ आना (haath aanaa)

verb A compound expression built on हाथ (haath, 'hand') plus आना (aanaa, 'to come'), used idiomatically for something falling into one's possession or grasp, often by luck or after effort rather than deliberate acquisition.
Sense 1 : to come into one's possession, to be obtained (often unexpectedly or after effort)
verb, intransitive The possessor is marked with को (ko), and the thing obtained is the grammatical subject that agrees with the verb: e.g. 'उसे नौकरी हाथ आई' (use naukrii haath aaii, 'he got hold of a job'). This is a dative-subject frame like लगना (lagnaa).
Synonyms: मिलना (milnaa), प्राप्त होना (praapt honaa, formal)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This phrase carries a flavor of fortune or fate: something 'comes into your hand' rather than you actively seizing it, which fits Hindi's broader habit of framing events as happening *to* a person (को (ko) constructions) rather than being driven *by* them. It is often used for opportunities, evidence, or rare finds slipping into someone's grasp -- 'मौका हाथ आना' (maukaa haath aanaa, 'an opportunity coming one's way') is a very common pairing.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often try to make the person who receives the thing the grammatical subject with ने (ne), but हाथ आना (haath aanaa) takes a को (ko)-marked possessor instead, with the thing obtained as the subject. 2) Because आना (aanaa) is intransitive, there is no ने (ne) marking anywhere in this construction, even though the meaning feels like 'getting' something.

Sense 2 : to be caught, to be captured (of a person or animal)
verb, intransitive
Synonyms: पकड़ में आना (pakad men aanaa), गिरफ़्तार होना (giraftaar honaa, formal, of a person by authorities)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: Used for a fugitive, thief, or fleeing animal finally being caught, as in crime reporting or storytelling: 'चोर पुलिस के हाथ आ गया' (chor pulis ke haath aa gayaa, 'the thief was caught by the police'). Note the के हाथ आना (ke haath aanaa) pattern here, distinct from the को (ko) pattern in the possession sense -- both share the same root idea of something landing in someone's 'hand.'
