राख (raakh)

noun A concrete noun meaning 'ash' -- the grey powder left after burning -- with a widely used figurative sense for destruction or ruin.
Sense 1 : ash, ashes
noun, feminine
Synonyms: भस्म (bhasm, literary/ritual)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: In a Delhi kitchen you might hear सिगरेट की राख (sigret kii raakh, 'cigarette ash') or हवन की राख (havan kii raakh, 'the ash from a havan/fire ritual') -- राख (raakh) is everyday but also carries ritual weight, since sacred ash from a havan or cremation pyre is treated with respect, sometimes even smeared on the forehead as भस्म (bhasm).
Common Mistakes: 1) राख (raakh) is used as a mass noun, much like English 'ash' -- learners often try to pluralize it (राखें raakhen) when talking about ash in general, which sounds odd; only use a plural form for distinct heaps or piles of ash in specific contexts.

(sigret kii raakh yahaan mat giraao।)

Sense 2 : ruin, destruction (figurative)
noun, feminine Common in the fixed expression राख हो जाना (raakh ho jaanaa, 'to be burnt/reduced to ashes'), used figuratively for a home, dream, or relationship being utterly destroyed.
Synonyms: बर्बादी (barbaadii), विनाश (vinaash)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This figurative sense leans on the same image English uses in 'burnt to a crisp' or 'in ashes' -- a house, a business, or even hopes can जलकर राख हो जाना (jalkar raakh ho jaanaa, 'burn down to ashes'), and you will hear this in news reports about fires as often as in poetic or emotional speech.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes translate 'my dreams turned to ashes' too literally; the natural Hindi idiom is X राख हो गया/गई (raakh ho gayaa, 'X turned to ash') or X जलकर राख हो गया (jalkar raakh ho gayaa, 'X burned down to ash'), not a word-for-word rendering.
