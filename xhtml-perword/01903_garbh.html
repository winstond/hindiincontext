गर्भ (garbh)

noun गर्भ (garbh) primarily means 'womb' or 'pregnancy,' and extends figuratively to mean the 'interior' or 'hidden depths' of something, as in गर्भ में (garbh men, 'inside/within').
Sense 1 : pregnancy
noun, masculine Common in set phrases such as गर्भ में होना (garbh men honaa, 'to be pregnant/in the womb') and गर्भवती (garbhavtii, 'pregnant woman'), where गर्भ (garbh) functions as the root idea of 'being with child.'
Synonyms: गर्भावस्था (garbhaavasthaa, more formal/medical)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This sense underlies everyday words like गर्भवती (garbhavtii, 'pregnant') and गर्भपात (garbhpaat, 'abortion/miscarriage') -- recognizing गर्भ (garbh) as their shared root helps you unlock a whole cluster of vocabulary around pregnancy and childbirth.

Sense 2 : womb
noun, masculine
Synonyms: कोख (kokh)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: गर्भ (garbh) is the more formal, almost medical/literary term for the womb, while कोख (kokh) carries a warmer, more intimate, everyday feel -- you will hear a mother's कोख (kokh) spoken of tenderly in conversation, but doctors and news reports use गर्भ (garbh).
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes confuse गर्भ (garbh) with पेट (pet, 'stomach/belly') -- गर्भ (garbh) specifically refers to the womb/uterus as a reproductive organ, not the belly in general.

Sense 3 : interior, hidden depths (figurative)
noun, masculine Used almost always in the construction X के गर्भ में (ke garbh men, 'in the womb/depths of X'), as in समय के गर्भ में (samay ke garbh men, 'in the womb of time') or धरती के गर्भ में (dhartii ke garbh men, 'in the depths of the earth').
Synonyms: अंदर (andar, less literary), भीतर (bhiitar, less literary)
Frequency: low
Cultural Note: This is a lovely piece of Hindi imagery: just as a womb holds something hidden and growing, धरती के गर्भ में (dhartii ke garbh men, 'in the womb of the earth') or समय के गर्भ में (samay ke garbh men, 'in the womb of time') both suggest something concealed, waiting to be revealed -- you will meet this often in news reports about archaeology or disasters.
Common Mistakes: 1) This figurative use is common in news and formal writing (e.g. earthquakes, buried history) but would sound overly poetic in casual chit-chat -- learners should not overuse it in everyday speech.
