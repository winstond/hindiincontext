माँग (maang)

noun Two main senses: 'demand' (economic/general, as in wanting something or in market demand) and 'the parting of the hair', a word tied specifically to married Hindu women's appearance. Both are feminine nouns; the verbal root माँगना (maangnaa, 'to ask for/request') is a related but separate lemma.
Sense 1 : demand (for something, including economic demand)
noun, feminine
Synonyms: मांग (maang, variant spelling without chandrabindu), जरूरत (jaroorat, broader: 'need')
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: You'll see this word constantly in news about strikes and protests -- कर्मचारियों की माँग (karmchaariyon kii maang, 'the employees' demand') is a staple headline phrase. It's also the standard economics term, so students encounter it early in both news Hindi and textbook Hindi.
Common Mistakes: 1) माँग (maang) is often paired with की पूर्ति (kii poorti, 'supply of') in economic contexts -- 'demand and supply' is माँग और पूर्ति (maang aur poorti). 2) Do not confuse with माँगना (maangnaa), the verb 'to ask for' -- माँग is the noun derived from it, used for 'a demand/request' as a thing, not the act of asking.

कर्मचारियों की माँग है कि वेतन बढ़ाया जाए। The employees' demand is that salaries be increased.
(karmchaariyon kii maang hai ki vetan badhaayaa jaae।)

Sense 2 : the parting of the hair (on the head)
noun, feminine
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: माँग में सिंदूर भरना (maang men sindoor bharnaa, 'to fill the parting with vermilion') is one of the central visual symbols of a married Hindu woman in North India -- you'll hear this phrase in wedding contexts, songs, and family conversation. It's a lovely example of how a very ordinary word (also meaning 'demand') carries deep cultural freight in its second sense.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes miss the cultural weight of this word: माँग भरना (maang bharnaa, 'to fill the parting') refers to the ritual of applying sindoor (vermilion) to the parting, a marker of being married -- it's not just a neutral description of a hairstyle.
