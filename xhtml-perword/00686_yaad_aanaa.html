याद आना (yaad aanaa)

verb A compound expression built from याद (yaad, 'memory/remembrance') plus आना (aanaa, 'to come'), used to say that something comes to mind or is recalled/missed -- an involuntary, dative-subject construction rather than an active 'remembering.'
Sense 1 : to recall, to come to mind
verb, intransitive Takes a को-marked experiencer as the logical subject, with the recalled thing as the grammatical subject: मुझे उसका नाम याद आया (mujhe uskaa naam yaad aayaa, 'I remembered his name', lit. 'to me his name came-to-memory'). Agreement is with the recalled thing, not the experiencer.
Synonyms: याद होना (yaad honaa, to have in memory, stative), स्मरण होना (smaran honaa, literary)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is a classic case of Hindi framing a mental event as something that 'arrives' at you rather than something you actively do -- much like नींद आना (niind aanaa, 'sleep comes') or गुस्सा आना (gussaa aanaa, 'anger comes'). It fits the broader pattern of body/mind-as-recipient verbs that take a को (ko)-marked experiencer, the same frame you've already seen with लगना (lagnaa) and पसंद आना (pasand aanaa).
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often try to make the experiencer the grammatical subject with ने (ne), producing *उसने याद आया (usne yaad aayaa); this verb never takes ने (ne) -- the experiencer is always को (ko)-marked. 2) Confusing this with याद करना (yaad karnaa, 'to try to remember, to recall deliberately') -- याद आना (yaad aanaa) is involuntary, something that just happens to you, while याद करना (yaad karnaa) is a willed act.

Sense 2 : to miss someone, to think of someone with longing
verb, intransitive The person missed is the grammatical subject (or appears as उसकी याद (uskii yaad, 'memory of him/her')), while the one doing the missing is को (ko)-marked: मुझे तुम्हारी याद आती है (mujhe tumhaarii yaad aatii hai, 'I miss you', lit. 'to me your memory comes').
Synonyms: याद सताना (yaad sataanaa, more poetic/intense, 'to be haunted by memory')
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is probably the phrase every Hindi learner ends up using the most, whether in a text message or a Bollywood song lyric -- तुम्हारी याद आती है (tumhaarii yaad aatii hai, 'I miss you') is emotionally central to how Hindi expresses longing, treating the missing person as an absence that 'visits' you rather than something you do to them.
Common Mistakes: 1) English speakers instinctively reach for a direct translation of 'I miss you' with a transitive verb structure; Hindi instead routes it through this dative-experiencer frame, so the person missed never takes ने (ne) or acts as an agent. 2) The habitual आती है (aatii hai) form is far more common than a one-off आया (aayaa) when talking about missing a person, since it describes a recurring feeling, not a single event.
