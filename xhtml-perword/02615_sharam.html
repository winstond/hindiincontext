शरम (sharam)

noun A feeling-noun covering shame, shyness, and modesty; also lives in the common verb-phrase idioms शरम आना (sharam aanaa) and शरम करना (sharam karnaa). This is a variant spelling of शर्म (sharm) -- both are heard constantly in Delhi-NCR speech.
Sense 1 : shame, embarrassment
noun, feminine Most commonly used with आना (aanaa) in the dative-subject frame: किसी को शरम आना (kisii ko sharam aanaa, 'someone to feel ashamed') -- the feeling is treated as something that 'comes to' the person, not something they actively do.
Synonyms: शर्म (sharm, variant form), लज्जा (lajjaa, literary), संकोच (sankoch)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is a classic dative-subject emotion word, just like लगना (lagnaa) or गुस्सा आना (gussaa aanaa) -- Hindi treats shame as an external force that visits you (शरम आना, 'shame arrives') rather than an internal state you occupy. You'll hear elders scold youngsters with शरम नहीं आती? (sharam nahiin aatii, 'have you no shame?') as a very common rebuke in Delhi-NCR households.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often say मैं शरम हूँ (main sharam hoon) trying to say 'I am ashamed' -- but Hindi doesn't equate the person with the feeling; you need तुम्हें/मुझे शरम आती है (mujhe sharam aatii hai, 'I feel ashamed') with को-marking, not है (hai) alone. 2) शरम करना (sharam karnaa) does NOT mean 'to feel ashamed' -- it means 'to be shy/reticent about doing something' or, scoldingly, 'to have some shame/decency' (कुछ तो शरम करो! (kuchh to sharam karo, 'have some shame!')).

Sense 2 : shyness, coyness, modesty
noun, feminine
Synonyms: हया (hayaa), संकोच (sankoch), झिझक (jhijhak)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: In traditional family settings, शरम (sharam) as modesty is often praised as a virtue, especially expected of a नई दुल्हन (naii dulhan, 'new bride') or young children in front of elders -- घूँघट (ghoonghat, 'veil') and lowered eyes are the visual shorthand for this kind of शरम (sharam) in North Indian culture.
Common Mistakes: 1) This sense overlaps with shame but is softer -- a new bride's शरम (sharam) at meeting in-laws, or a child's शरम (sharam) around strangers, is bashfulness, not guilt; context (not grammar) distinguishes the two senses.
