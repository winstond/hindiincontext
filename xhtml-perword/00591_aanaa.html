आना (aanaa)

verb The basic verb 'to come'; also functions as part of many involuntary/experiential expressions (नींद आना (niind aanaa, 'to feel sleepy'), याद आना (yaad aanaa, 'to come to mind')) and as an auxiliary marking learned ability (हिंदी आना (hindii aanaa, 'to know Hindi')). All are covered as separate senses below.
Sense 1 : to come, arrive (physical motion)
verb, intransitive
Synonyms: पहुँचना (pahunchnaa, to arrive)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is one of the first verbs every learner meets, paired constantly with जाना (jaanaa, 'to go') -- Hindi speakers use the आना/जाना (aanaa/jaanaa) pair the way English uses 'come/go,' but always oriented to the speaker's own position, so getting the direction right takes a little ear-training.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often try to make this transitive with ने (ne) in the past tense; आना (aanaa) is intransitive, so the subject stays unmarked: वह आया (vah aayaa, 'he came'), never उसने आया (usne aayaa).

Sense 2 : to know how to, be able to (learned skill)
verb, intransitive Used with the skill as subject and the knower marked with को (ko): मुझे हिंदी आती है (mujhe hindii aatii hai, 'I know Hindi'). The verb agrees with the skill, not with the person.
Synonyms: जानना (jaannaa, to know facts, not skills)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is the classic dative-subject frame: Hindi treats knowing a skill as something that 'comes to' you rather than something you 'do,' the same logic behind नींद आना (niind aanaa) and पसंद आना (pasand aanaa). Ask someone आपको हिंदी आती है? (aapko hindii aatii hai?, 'do you know Hindi?') and you'll hear this constantly.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often mark the knower with ने (ne) by analogy with other verbs; this sense always takes को (ko), never ने (ne). 2) The verb agrees with the skill (हिंदी आती है (hindii aatii hai)), not with the person who knows it -- a common gender/number slip.
Sample Sentences:

Sense 3 : to occur, arise (of feelings, states, ideas -- involuntary experience)
verb, intransitive Forms fixed pairings with abstract nouns where the experiencer is marked with को (ko) and the noun is the grammatical subject: गुस्सा आना (gussaa aanaa, 'to get angry'), याद आना (yaad aanaa, 'to come to mind'), नींद आना (niind aanaa, 'to feel sleepy'), डर आना (dar aanaa, 'to feel afraid').
Synonyms: उठना (uthnaa, to arise, of emotions)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is Hindi's 'body/mind as receiver' logic in full force -- feelings and states are treated as visitors that arrive at you rather than actions you perform, which is why मुझे गुस्सा आया (mujhe gussaa aayaa, 'I got angry') literally reads as 'anger came to me.' Once this clicks, dozens of common expressions suddenly make sense together.
Common Mistakes: 1) English speakers instinctively want to be the subject ('I remember,' 'I feel sleepy') and use ने (ne) or make themselves the agent; Hindi makes the feeling itself the subject and the person merely the को (ko)-marked experiencer, so word order and agreement follow the emotion, not the person.
Sample Sentences:

Sense 4 : to appear, occur (in writing, exams, lists, etc.)
verb, intransitive
Synonyms: दिखना (dikhnaa, to appear visually)
