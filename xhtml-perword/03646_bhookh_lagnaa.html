भूख लगना (bhookh lagnaa)

verb A body-as-experiencer construction meaning 'to feel hungry,' literally 'hunger to attach/strike (someone).' Also used figuratively for a strong craving or appetite for something non-food related.
Sense 1 : to feel hungry
verb, intransitive Person who is hungry is marked with को (ko), e.g. मुझे भूख लगी है (mujhe bhookh lagii hai, 'I am hungry'). भूख (bhookh) is the grammatical subject and agrees with the verb; the experiencer is a dative-marked non-agent, so ने (ne) is never used here.
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Hindi treats hunger, like sleep (नींद आना, niind aanaa) and fear (डर लगना, dar lagnaa), as something that happens to you rather than something you actively feel -- the body is on the receiving end, not the agent. This is a great early example for learners of the 'dative subject' pattern they have studied with लगना (lagnaa) and चाहिए (chaahie): the experiencer takes को (ko) and the real grammatical subject is the sensation itself.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often try to say मैं भूखा हूँ (main bhookhaa hoon) as if translating 'I am hungry' directly with the adjective भूखा (bhookhaa) -- this is grammatical but less natural in casual speech than मुझे भूख लगी है (mujhe bhookh lagii hai), which describes hunger as something that arrives. 2) Learners sometimes wrongly add ने (ne) to the experiencer, producing मैंने भूख लगी (mainne bhookh lagii), which is incorrect -- the experiencer always takes को (ko), never ने (ne).

Sense 2 : to develop a craving or strong desire (figurative)
verb, intransitive Used with an oblique noun phrase plus की भूख लगना (kii bhookh lagnaa) to express craving for something abstract, e.g. शोहरत की भूख लगना (shohrat kii bhookh lagnaa, 'to hunger for fame').
Synonyms: तरसना (tarasnaa, to yearn), लालायित होना (laalaayit honaa, literary)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: Just as English speakers say someone has 'a hunger for power,' Hindi extends भूख (bhookh) the same way -- power, fame, knowledge, or money can all be objects of भूख (bhookh), showing how naturally the appetite metaphor crosses into ambition and desire in both languages.
