के आगे (ke aage)

postposition A compound postposition built from आगे (aage, 'ahead/in front') governing the oblique with के (ke); covers physical positioning 'in front of' and the figurative sense of being powerless or humbled 'before' someone or something.
Sense 1 : in front of (physical location)
postposition
Synonyms: के सामने (ke saamne)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Think of standing in a queue: घर के आगे (ghar ke aage, 'in front of the house') describes the space right outside the door, the way you'd picture a scooter parked there or kids playing cricket in that patch of street.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes use के सामने (ke saamne) and के आगे (ke aage) as fully interchangeable; के सामने (ke saamne) is the more neutral choice for 'facing/opposite,' while के आगे (ke aage) more often implies something is just ahead or blocking the path, not simply facing it.
Sample Sentences:
घर के आगे एक गाड़ी खड़ी है। A car is standing in front of the house.
(ghar ke aage ek gaadii khadii hai।)

Sense 2 : before/in the face of (figurative helplessness or comparison)
postposition Commonly used with verbs of helplessness such as झुकना (jhuknaa, 'to bow') or हार मानना (haar maannaa, 'to admit defeat') to express being overpowered by someone or something.
Synonyms: के सामने (ke saamne), की तुलना में (kii tulnaa men, for comparison sense)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is the phrase behind lines like किस्मत के आगे (kismat ke aage, 'before fate') that show up often in film dialogue and everyday fatalistic remarks -- Hindi frames fate, love, or elders as forces one stands 'in front of' and simply cannot resist, which fits the language's broader habit of treating powerful abstractions as agents.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often miss the figurative extension and only apply के आगे (ke aage) to physical space; in sentences like किस्मत के आगे किसी की नहीं चलती (kismat ke aage kisii kii nahiin chaltii, 'no one's will prevails before fate'), it signals powerlessness, not location. 2) In comparisons, यह उसके आगे कुछ नहीं (yah uske aage kuchh nahiin, 'this is nothing compared to that') uses के आगे (ke aage) to mean 'next to/compared to,' which can confuse learners expecting only a spatial reading.
