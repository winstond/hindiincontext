असर पड़ना (asar padnaa)

verb A compound expression meaning 'to have an effect' or 'to be affected/influenced' -- built from असर (asar, 'effect') plus पड़ना (padnaa, 'to fall'), using Hindi's common 'effect falls (on someone/something)' frame.
Sense 1 : to be affected/influenced (by something)
verb, intransitive The person or thing affected is marked with पर (par) or पे (pe, colloquial), e.g. 'उस पर असर पड़ा' (us par asar padaa, 'it had an effect on him'); the cause/source, if mentioned, is marked with से (se).
Synonyms: प्रभाव पड़ना (prabhaav padnaa, more formal), फ़र्क़ पड़ना (farq padnaa, 'to make a difference')
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This phrase treats an 'effect' as something that literally 'falls' on a person, much like बारिश पड़ना (baarish padnaa, 'rain falls') -- a good example of Hindi's love of impersonal, event-as-agent framing where things happen *to* you rather than you doing them. You'll hear it constantly in advice-giving: 'उसकी बातों का असर पड़ा' (uskii baaton kaa asar padaa, 'his words had an effect').
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often try to make असर (asar) the direct object with a transitive verb like करना (karnaa), but the standard idiom is intransitive: असर पड़ता है (asar padtaa hai), not असर करता है done to a person -- असर करना (asar karnaa) exists but shifts the subject onto the thing causing the effect. 2) The affected party takes पर (par), not को (ko) as a learner might expect by analogy with दर्द होना (dard honaa)-type constructions.

Sense 2 : to be impacted negatively (health, performance, condition)
verb, intransitive Common with body parts or abstract conditions as the subject of पर (par): 'सेहत पर असर पड़ा' (sehat par asar padaa, 'health was affected').
Synonyms: बिगड़ना (bigadnaa, 'to worsen', narrower scope), नुक़सान होना (nuqsaan honaa, 'to be harmed')
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is the phrase you'll hear from worried parents and doctors alike: पढ़ाई का असर आँखों पर पड़ता है (padhaaii kaa asar aankhon par padtaa hai, 'studying takes a toll on the eyes'). It's really the same core sense as the general one, narrowed by context to a negative outcome -- Hindi doesn't need a separate verb for 'to suffer harm,' it just lets context darken this same idiom.
