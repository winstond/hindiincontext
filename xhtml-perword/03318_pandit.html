पंडित (pandit)

noun Primarily a noun for a learned Brahmin scholar or priest, extended informally to mean any knowledgeable expert; also used as an honorific title before names.
Sense 1 : Hindu priest or scholar of Sanskrit/scripture
noun, masculine
Synonyms: पुरोहित (purohit), आचार्य (aachaary, formal), ब्राह्मण (braahman, refers to caste rather than role)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: You'll hear this word used to call the family priest who comes to conduct weddings, naming ceremonies, or havan (havan) rituals at home -- calling him Panditji (pandit jii) is standard, respectful address. Jawaharlal Nehru was popularly called Pandit Nehru (pandit nehroo) because of his Kashmiri Brahmin background, showing how the title sticks to a family lineage even outside religious contexts.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes assume पंडित (pandit) always means a temple priest performing rituals, but it more precisely denotes someone learned in Sanskrit texts and scriptures; the person who performs your puja (poojaa) at home might be called this even if their main expertise is ritual knowledge rather than temple service. 2) Don't confuse with पुजारी (pujaarii), which specifically means the priest who tends a temple's deity -- पंडित (pandit) is broader and carries more of a scholarly connotation.

Sense 2 : an expert or highly knowledgeable person (often ironic)
noun, masculine
Synonyms: विशेषज्ञ (visheshagy, formal), उस्ताद (ustaad, colloquial)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This is the word behind the common jab अपने आपको बड़ा पंडित समझते हो (apne aapko badaa pandit samajhte ho, 'you think you're such a big expert') -- said with an eye-roll to someone being a know-it-all, much like English 'Mr. Know-It-All.' It plays on the respect the religious sense commands, flipped into gentle mockery.
Common Mistakes: 1) This extended sense is often lightly sarcastic or teasing in tone -- calling a friend बड़े पंडित बने हो (bade pandit bane ho, 'acting like a big expert, are you') pokes fun at someone showing off knowledge, so learners should be careful not to use it as a flat, sincere compliment in every context.
