हीरो (hiiro)

noun An English loanword used in two everyday senses: the male lead of a film, and (often teasingly) a general term for a dashing or heroic man. Inline Hindi as Devanagari (placeholder).
Sense 1 : male lead actor in a film
noun, masculine
Synonyms: नायक (naayak, formal)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is straight out of Bollywood chatter -- ask any Delhi teenager who the हीरो (hiiro) of a new film is, and they will never say नायक (naayak). The word has fully naturalized into Hindi film talk, sitting alongside हीरोइन (hiiroin) and विलन (vilan) as the standard cast vocabulary.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes reach for नायक (naayak) by default because it is the 'dictionary' word, but in casual conversation about films हीरो (hiiro) is what people actually say -- नायक (naayak) sounds like something from a review or textbook. 2) The female counterpart is हीरोइन (hiiroin), not a feminine form of हीरो (hiiro) itself -- हीरो (hiiro) stays invariant regardless of who is being discussed generically.

Sense 2 : a heroic or dashing man (general/figurative use)
noun, masculine
Synonyms: बहादुर (bahaadur, emphasizes courage rather than dash)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: A mother scolding a son who did something reckless might say, तुम कोई हीरो नहीं हो (tum koii hiiro nahiin ho, 'you're no hero'), or friends might rib someone acting overconfident as अपने को हीरो समझता है (apne ko hiiro samajhtaa hai, 'he thinks he's such a hero'). This figurative use grows directly from the film sense -- the swagger of the film हीरो (hiiro) is projected onto real life, sometimes sincerely, often with a wink.
Common Mistakes: 1) Often used sarcastically or teasingly rather than as sincere praise -- calling someone बड़ा हीरो बनता है (badaa hiiro bantaa hai, 'he's acting like such a big hero') mocks someone for showing off or taking a needless risk, which can trip up learners expecting only admiration.
