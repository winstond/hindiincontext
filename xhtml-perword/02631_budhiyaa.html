बुढ़िया (budhiyaa)

noun/adjective An old woman (budhiyaa) -- the everyday feminine noun for an elderly woman, and by extension a colloquial adjective describing something old/worn. Related to बूढ़ा (boodhaa, old man/old-adjective, masculine).
Sense 1 : old woman
noun, feminine
Synonyms: वृद्धा (vriddhaa, formal), बुज़ुर्ग औरत (buzurg aurat)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Every Hindi speaker grew up on stories that open with एक बुढ़िया थी (ek budhiyaa thii, 'there was an old woman') -- it is the classic folk-tale character, often clever, poor, or a bit cranky, similar to the 'old woman who lived in a shoe' archetype in English tales. In real conversation it is used casually and can carry either affection or mild disrespect depending on context and tone of voice.
Common Mistakes: 1) बुढ़िया (budhiyaa) can sound blunt or even slightly dismissive depending on tone -- in polite address or formal writing, वृद्धा (vriddhaa) or बुज़ुर्ग महिला (buzurg mahilaa) is safer; बुढ़िया (budhiyaa) is fine in casual storytelling or when referring to a stranger informally, but avoid using it to a woman's face unless the relationship is very familiar.

(ek budhiyaa thii, jo jangal ke paas ek chhotii sii jhopdii men rahtii thii।)

Sense 2 : old, worn-out (of a thing, colloquial)
adjective Used informally to describe an object as old and decrepit, by loose extension from the noun sense; the more standard and widely used adjective for 'old' (of things) is पुराना (puraanaa).
Synonyms: पुराना (puraanaa), जर्जर (jarjar, literary/formal)
Frequency: low
Cultural Note: You might hear someone jokingly call a rickety old car or a rundown building बुढ़िया गाड़ी (budhiyaa gaadii, 'an old, worn-out car') -- it borrows the noun's sense of frailty and age, said with a wry, affectionate tone rather than literal precision.
Common Mistakes: 1) This adjectival use is colloquial and less common than पुराना (puraanaa) -- learners should recognize it if heard but default to पुराना (puraanaa) for their own active speech when describing an old object.
