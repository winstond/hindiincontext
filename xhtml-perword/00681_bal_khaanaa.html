बल खाना (bal khaanaa)

verb A compound expression built on बल (bal, 'twist/force') plus खाना (khaanaa) used as a light verb; it describes something physically coiling or twisting, and by extension a person or thing being contorted or evasive.
Sense 1 : to coil, twist, or writhe (of a physical thing)
verb, intransitive Subject is typically something long and flexible -- a rope, wire, snake, or body part -- that forms coils or bends under its own tension.
Synonyms: मुड़ना (mudnaa), ऐंठना (ainthnaa)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This is one of a whole family of Hindi expressions where खाना (khaanaa) means 'to undergo' rather than 'to eat' -- compare धोखा खाना (dhokhaa khaanaa, 'to be deceived') or मार खाना (maar khaanaa, 'to take a beating'). Once a learner spots this pattern, a lot of otherwise odd-sounding verb phrases suddenly make sense.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes try to use this only for people being evasive, but the core, most common use is purely physical -- a rope या (yaa) wire or a snake literally coiling: साँप बल खा रहा था (saamp bal khaa rahaa thaa, 'the snake was writhing/coiling'). 2) खाना (khaanaa) here is a light verb contributing an idiomatic 'undergo/take on' sense, not its literal 'eat' meaning -- learners fresh off learning खाना (khaanaa) as 'to eat' can be thrown by this.

Sense 2 : to squirm, be contorted, or act evasively (of a person, figuratively)
verb, intransitive Used figuratively for a person's body language (squirming with discomfort) or for speech/behavior that twists to dodge a direct answer.
Synonyms: बचना (bachnaa, to avoid), पेच खाना (pech khaanaa, less common)
Frequency: low
Cultural Note: Picture someone caught in an awkward question at a family gathering, twisting in their chair and dodging with vague answers -- that physical image of a body 'coiling' is exactly what this figurative sense borrows from the literal one.
Common Mistakes: 1) This figurative sense needs context to land -- बल खाना (bal khaanaa) alone without a discomfort/evasion context will usually just read as the literal physical-coiling sense. 2) It is not used to mean simple lying; it is more about squirming or wriggling around a point, closer to English 'to hem and haw' or 'to writhe out of.'

(jab bhii usse uskii tanakhvaah ke baare men poochho, vah bal khaane lagtaa hai।)
