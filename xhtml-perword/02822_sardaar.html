सरदार (sardaar)

noun Primarily means a leader or chief, but in everyday Delhi-NCR speech it is most often heard as the common term for a Sikh man, especially one wearing a turban (pagdii).
Sense 1 : a Sikh man (especially turbaned)
noun, masculine
Synonyms: सरदार जी (sardaar jii, respectful form of address)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Walk through any Delhi market and you will hear सरदार जी (sardaar jii) used as a warm, respectful way to address a Sikh shopkeeper or taxi driver -- it is not a formal title but a familiar, everyday form of address, much like calling someone 'sir' with a specific ethnic warmth. The word also anchors a whole genre of (sometimes unfortunately stereotypical) Sardar jokes in North Indian pop culture, so learners should be aware of the associated cultural baggage.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes assume सरदार (sardaar) is a surname or caste name; it functions more like a respectful descriptor referring to Sikh identity, often paired with जी (jii) as सरदार जी (sardaar jii) when addressing someone directly. 2) Using this term for a non-Sikh person can come across as odd or mistaken, since the association with Sikh identity (and often the turban) is strong in everyday usage.

Sense 2 : leader, chief, headman
noun, masculine
Synonyms: मुखिया (mukhiyaa), नेता (netaa), प्रमुख (pramukh, somewhat formal)
Frequency: low
Cultural Note: This is the older, root meaning -- सरदार (sardaar) comes from Persian roots meaning 'head of a group,' historically used for military or tribal chiefs and heads of caravans. You will still meet it in historical contexts or set phrases like मज़दूरों का सरदार (mazdooron kaa sardaar, 'the leader of the laborers'), but in casual conversation today the Sikh-identity sense (above) is what people reach for first.
