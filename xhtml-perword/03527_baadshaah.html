बादशाह (baadshaah)

noun Primarily a noun meaning 'king/emperor', with a common figurative use for someone who lives like a king or is the best/master at something.
Sense 1 : king, emperor, monarch
noun, masculine
Synonyms: राजा (raajaa), सम्राट (samraat, more formal/historical), शहंशाह (shahanshaah, emphasizes 'king of kings')
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This word comes from Persian and is the term historically used for Mughal emperors -- think Akbar or Shah Jahan being called बादशाह (baadshaah) rather than राजा (raajaa). In everyday speech, calling someone 'raja' feels folk-taleish, while 'badshah' feels grander and more historically specific.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes use राजा (raajaa) and बादशाह (baadshaah) as perfectly interchangeable -- बादशाह (baadshaah) carries a specifically Mughal/Islamic-courtly flavor in most contexts, so it shows up more in historical or Mughal-era references than in fairy tales, where राजा (raajaa) is the default.

Sense 2 : the best, a master (figurative), one who lives lavishly
noun, masculine Used predicatively or as an exclamation of praise, often in phrases like किसी का बादशाह होना (kisii kaa baadshaah honaa, 'to be the king/master of something').
Synonyms: उस्ताद (ustaad, master of a craft), शहंशाह (shahanshaah, grander/more emphatic)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: You'll hear this a lot in Delhi banter -- a friend who pays the bill without blinking or nails a tough task gets called बादशाह (baadshaah) as a warm, half-joking compliment, similar to calling someone 'the man' or 'boss' in English.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners may miss that this sense is casual praise, not literal royalty -- calling a friend बादशाह (baadshaah) after they ace an exam or treat everyone to dinner is a compliment about swagger or generosity, not a claim about kingship.
