वचन (vachan)

noun Primarily a formal noun meaning 'promise, word (given)' -- as in giving one's word -- and, in a completely separate grammatical sense, the technical term for 'number' (singular/plural) in Hindi grammar.
Sense 1 : a solemn promise or word given
noun, masculine
Synonyms: वादा (vaadaa), प्रतिज्ञा (pratigyaa, formal)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This word carries real weight -- it shows up in stories about kings and sages keeping their वचन (vachan) even at great cost, and in phrases like राम का वचन (raam kaa vachan, 'Ram's word') where breaking one's word is treated as a moral failure. In daily life you'll hear it most in slightly formal or emotional contexts -- a parent extracting a serious promise from a child, or a hero in a film swearing one.
Common Mistakes: 1) वचन (vachan) sounds more solemn and formal than वादा (vaadaa) -- it's used for weighty commitments (a vow to a parent, a promise in a story or scripture), not casual promises like 'I'll call you back.' For everyday promises, वादा (vaadaa) is the natural choice. 2) The common phrase is वचन देना (vachan denaa, 'to give one's word') or वचन तोड़ना (vachan todnaa, 'to break one's word') -- learners sometimes reach for करना (karnaa) instead of देना (denaa) here.

Sense 2 : grammatical number (singular/plural)
noun, masculine Used almost exclusively in the fixed grammatical terms एकवचन (ekavchan, 'singular') and बहुवचन (bahuvchan, 'plural').
Frequency: low
Cultural Note: You'll meet this sense in Hindi grammar class rather than in conversation -- it's the same वचन (vachan) as 'word/promise,' extended to mean the 'form' a noun takes, singular or plural. Think of it as 'how the word is spoken' -- one form or many.
Sample Sentences:
हिंदी में वचन दो प्रकार के होते हैं -- एकवचन और बहुवचन। In Hindi, grammatical number is of two kinds -- singular and plural.
(hindii men vachan do prakaar ke hote hain -- ekavchan aur bahuvchan।)
