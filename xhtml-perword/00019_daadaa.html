दादा (daadaa)

noun Most commonly means 'paternal grandfather'; also lives on in the common colloquial phrase for a neighborhood tough guy or gang leader, e.g. मोहल्ले का दादा (mohalle kaa daadaa, 'the neighborhood tough guy').
Sense 1 : paternal grandfather
noun, masculine
Synonyms: पितामह (pitaamah, literary)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: In most North Indian households, दादा (daadaa) and दादी (daadii) are the grandparents you typically live with or near, since traditional families are patrilocal -- so these words often carry a warmer, more everyday-present feel than नाना (naanaa) and नानी (naanii), who are visited during holidays.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often use दादा (daadaa) for any grandfather, but Hindi distinguishes sides of the family: दादा (daadaa) is specifically the father's father, while नाना (naanaa) is the mother's father -- mixing them up is a classic learner slip. 2) The paired term for grandmother is दादी (daadii), not a feminine form of दादा (daadaa) itself.

Sense 2 : local tough guy, gang leader, bully (figurative)
noun, masculine Almost always used in a fixed phrase naming the turf or group, like मोहल्ले का दादा (mohalle kaa daadaa, 'the neighborhood tough guy') or इलाके का दादा (ilaake kaa daadaa, 'the local don').
Synonyms: गुंडा (gundaa), भाई (bhaaii, colloquial, 'boss/don' sense), दादागिरी (daadaagirii, related noun meaning 'bullying/throwing one's weight around')
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This sense is everywhere in Hindi cinema and everyday banter -- a मोहल्ले का दादा (mohalle kaa daadaa) is the guy everyone in the lane is a little afraid of, and दादागिरी (daadaagirii) (the related abstract noun) means 'throwing your weight around' in that same swaggering way.
Common Mistakes: 1) This sense only works with a 'territory' attached (मोहल्ले का (mohalle kaa), कॉलेज का (kolej kaa)) -- calling someone just दादा (daadaa) alone in this sense sounds incomplete to a native ear; it needs the 'king of ___' frame to land as 'tough guy' rather than 'grandfather.'
