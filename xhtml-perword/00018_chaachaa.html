चाचा (chaachaa)

noun चाचा (chaachaa) is a core kinship term for your father's younger brother, and it doubles as a warm, respectful way to address an older man who isn't actually family.
Sense 1 : father's younger brother (paternal uncle)
noun, masculine
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: In most North Indian families the father's brothers are ranked by age relative to him, which is why Hindi needs two separate words (चाचा (chaachaa) and ताऊ (taaoo)) where English just says 'uncle' -- the family tree is baked right into the vocabulary. Children often address and refer to their चाचा (chaachaa) with the same word directly, sometimes softened to चाचू (chaachoo) at home.
Common Mistakes: 1) Hindi kinship terms are specific, not generic like English 'uncle': चाचा (chaachaa) is only your father's *younger* brother, ताऊ (taaoo) is his *elder* brother, and मामा (maamaa) is your mother's brother -- mixing these up is a classic learner slip. 2) The feminine counterpart (your चाचा's wife) is a separate word, चाची (chaachii), not a feminine inflection of चाचा (chaachaa).

Sense 2 : respectful/affectionate term of address for an older man (not a relative)
noun, masculine Used vocatively or as a polite third-person reference for a man roughly your father's age or older, much like calling an unrelated adult 'uncle' in Indian English.
Synonyms: अंकल (ankal, common with English-medium speakers), भाई साहब (bhaaii saahab, for someone closer to one's own generation)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This extended, affectionate use is why Jawaharlal Nehru was popularly nicknamed 'Chacha Nehru' (chaachaa nehroo) by generations of Indian schoolchildren -- the word carries a built-in warmth that 'uncle' alone doesn't quite capture in English.
Common Mistakes: 1) Don't reach for चाचा (chaachaa) for every older man -- it fits a shopkeeper, neighbor, or rickshaw-wala who feels like family-adjacent, while a stranger in a formal setting is better addressed as सर (sar) or साहब (saahab).
