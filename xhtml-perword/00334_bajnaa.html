बजना (bajnaa)

verb The intransitive partner of बजाना (bajaanaa, 'to play/ring something'); बजना (bajnaa) describes a sound-making instrument, bell, or alarm sounding on its own, and also the clock striking a given hour.
Sense 1 : to ring, sound (of an instrument, bell, alarm)
verb, intransitive
Synonyms: गूँजना (goonjnaa, to resound/echo)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Think of बजना (bajnaa) and बजाना (bajaanaa) as a classic transitive/intransitive pair, much like खुलना (khulnaa)/खोलना (kholnaa): कोई तबला बजाता है (koii tablaa bajaataa hai, 'someone plays the tabla'), but तबला खुद बजता नहीं (tablaa khud bajtaa nahiin, 'the tabla doesn't ring by itself') -- so बजना (bajnaa) is what happens when the sound just occurs, with no player mentioned.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often reach for बजाना (bajaanaa) when the subject is the instrument itself doing the sounding, not a person playing it -- घंटी बज रही है (ghantii baj rahii hai, 'the bell is ringing') takes बजना (bajnaa), not बजाना (bajaanaa), because no one is named as the player. 2) Since बजना (bajnaa) is intransitive, its subject never takes ने (ne) in the past: घंटी बजी (ghantii bajii, 'the bell rang'), not a ने-marked form.
Sample Sentences:
घंटी बज रही है, कोई दरवाज़े पर है। The bell is ringing, someone is at the door.
(ghantii baj rahii hai, koii darvaaze par hai।)

Sense 2 : to strike (of a clock, indicating time)
verb, intransitive Used impersonally with a time expression as subject: तीन बज गए हैं (tiin baj gae hain, 'it has struck three / it's three o'clock').
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This sense is really the clock-striking meaning extended to everyday timekeeping -- Hindi treats the hour itself as the thing doing the striking, so आठ बज गए (aath baj gae, 'it's eight o'clock') literally means 'eight have struck', a nice example of Hindi framing time as something that happens rather than something that simply is.
Common Mistakes: 1) Telling time in Hindi uses बजना (bajnaa) where English uses 'to be': कितने बजे हैं? (kitne baje hain?, 'what time is it?') literally asks 'how many have struck', which feels backwards to English speakers expecting a form of 'to be'.
