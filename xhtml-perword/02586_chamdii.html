चमड़ी (chamdii)

noun Refers to human or animal skin, mainly in its living, natural sense (as opposed to चमड़ा (chamdaa), the tanned/leather sense); also lives inside a few common idioms about stinginess and getting a scolding.
Sense 1 : skin (of a person or animal)
noun, feminine
Synonyms: त्वचा (tvachaa, formal/medical), खाल (khaal, often for animal hide)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Ask a Delhi mother about a child who fell off a bike and she'll say उसकी चमड़ी छिल गई (uskii chamdii chhil gaii, 'his skin got scraped'), not त्वचा (tvachaa) -- चमड़ी (chamdii) is the everyday, homely word for skin, while त्वचा (tvachaa) shows up on shampoo bottles and in doctors' clinics.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often reach for चमड़ा (chamdaa) when they mean living skin -- चमड़ी (chamdii) is the word for the skin on a body, while चमड़ा (chamdaa) is processed leather/hide. 2) त्वचा (tvachaa) is the more clinical or beauty-product word (as in a dermatologist's office or a cream ad); चमड़ी (chamdii) is what you'd say at home about a scraped knee or sunburnt arms.

Sense 2 : skin as a stand-in for a harsh beating or scolding (idiomatic)
noun, feminine Appears almost exclusively in fixed threatening expressions such as चमड़ी उतार देना (chamdii utaar denaa, 'to skin someone alive' -- i.e. to punish severely) and चमड़ी खींचना (chamdii khiinchnaa).
Synonyms: खाल (khaal, used in similar threats)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This is classic Indian-parent hyperbole: चमड़ी उतार दूँगा (chamdii utaar doongaa) is the kind of line every kid in Delhi has heard after coming home late, right up there with जान से मार दूँगा (jaan se maar doongaa) in the family repertoire of empty-but-effective threats.
Common Mistakes: 1) This sense is only used in exaggerated threats, not literal descriptions -- a parent saying चमड़ी उतार दूँगा (chamdii utaar doongaa, 'I'll skin you alive') is scolding, not describing actual violence, so learners should read it as hyperbole, not a literal warning.
