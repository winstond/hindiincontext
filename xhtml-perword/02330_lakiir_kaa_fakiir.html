लकीर का फ़कीर (lakiir kaa fakiir)

noun A fixed idiomatic phrase describing someone who blindly follows old ways or tradition without question -- literally 'a fakir (devotee) of the line.' It functions as a noun phrase, usually used predicatively to describe a person's character.
Sense 1 : a person who blindly follows tradition/convention, a slave to old ways
noun, masculine Used with होना (honaa) or बनना (bannaa): कोई लकीर का फ़कीर होता है (koii lakiir kaa fakiir hotaa hai, 'someone is a slave to convention'). Functions as a noun even though it describes a quality, similar to calling someone 'a creature of habit' in English.
Synonyms: रूढ़िवादी (roodhivaadii, focuses on conservatism specifically), दकियानूसी (dakiyaanoosii, old-fashioned in outlook)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: The image comes from a well-known fable about a fakir (ascetic) who drew a line for his disciples to follow and, after his death, his followers kept walking the exact same path even when it no longer made sense -- because that's 'the line the fakir drew.' Delhi speakers use this phrase a lot for stubborn relatives, bureaucrats, or institutions that follow outdated rules 'just because that's how it's always been done.' It pairs well with the adjective रूढ़िवादी (roodhivaadii) but carries more of a folksy, storytelling flavor than that more clinical-sounding word.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes try to use this as a straightforward adjective before a noun (like लकीर का फ़कीर आदमी (lakiir kaa fakiir aadmii)); it is far more natural as a predicate describing someone, e.g. वह लकीर का फ़कीर है (vah lakiir kaa fakiir hai, 'he is a slave to old ways'), rather than modifying a noun directly. 2) Do not translate word-for-word -- लकीर (lakiir) here means 'a line drawn (in the ground)' referring to old, fixed paths of habit, not a literal line or streak.
