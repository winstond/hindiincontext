उधार (udhaar)

noun/adverb Covers 'loan/credit' as a noun and 'on credit/borrowed' as an adverb/adjective-like usage -- both senses share the same everyday money-and-shopkeeper world.
Sense 1 : a loan; money or goods lent/borrowed
noun, masculine
Synonyms: क़र्ज़ (qarz), कर्ज़ (karz, variant spelling without nuqta), ऋण (rin, formal)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Every neighborhood kirana store in Delhi has a khaata (khaataa) -- a little ledger -- where the shopkeeper notes down उधार (udhaar) for regular customers who buy 'on the book' and settle up at month's end. It is a deeply normal, trust-based part of local shopping culture, not a shameful thing the way 'debt' can feel in English.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often reach for क़र्ज़ (qarz) by default, but उधार (udhaar) is the everyday word for small, informal borrowing (from a shop, a neighbor, a friend) -- क़र्ज़ (qarz) leans toward bigger, more formal debt like a bank loan. 2) The verb pairing matters: you 'give' or 'take' उधार (udhaar) with देना (denaa) and लेना (lenaa) -- उधार देना (udhaar denaa, 'to lend') and उधार लेना (udhaar lenaa, 'to borrow') -- rather than inventing a separate verb for lending/borrowing.

Sense 2 : on credit, on loan (describing how something is given/taken)
adverb Functions adverbially after verbs like देना (denaa), लेना (lenaa), and मिलना (milnaa) to describe the manner of the transaction, e.g. उधार मिलना (udhaar milnaa, 'to get on credit').
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: You will hear this constantly in market bargaining: सामान उधार दे दो (saamaan udhaar de do, 'give me the goods on credit') is a common, casual request between people who know each other, reflecting how much everyday commerce still runs on personal trust rather than cards or apps.
Common Mistakes: 1) This word does not inflect like a regular adjective -- you cannot say उधारा (udhaaraa) or उधारी चीज़ (udhaarii chiiz) for 'a borrowed thing'; उधार (udhaar) stays fixed and simply modifies the verb of giving/taking/getting.
