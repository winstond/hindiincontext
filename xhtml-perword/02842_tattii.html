टट्टी (tattii)

noun A everyday, somewhat informal word centered on human excrement and the act of defecating; also carries an older, unrelated sense of a woven screen or partition used as a blind or fence.
Sense 1 : feces, stool
noun, feminine
Synonyms: मल (mal, clinical/formal), गू (goo, colloquial, blunter)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is the word every Indian parent uses when asking a toddler about their business -- टट्टी करी? (tattii karii?, 'did you poop?') is a classic line in households across Delhi-NCR. It sits comfortably in the middle register: earthy and direct without being crude, unlike its blunter cousin गू (goo).
Common Mistakes: 1) This word is fine in casual and family speech, especially with children, but is too blunt for polite or formal contexts -- use मल (mal) or a euphemism like शौच (shauch) when speaking with doctors or in formal writing. 2) Learners sometimes treat it as vulgar slang, but among family and close friends, especially about kids or pets, it is completely normal and not at all shocking.

Sense 2 : the act of defecating
noun, feminine Used with करना (karnaa) as टट्टी करना (tattii karnaa) to mean 'to defecate.'
Synonyms: शौच (shauch, formal/clinical), संडास जाना (sandaas jaanaa, colloquial phrase)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: You will hear this constantly in households with small kids or in rural/small-town contexts where toilet training and outdoor sanitation habits are frequent topics of daily conversation.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes confuse this with पेशाब (peshaab, 'urine/to urinate') -- टट्टी (tattii) is specifically about solid waste, not liquid.

Sense 3 : a woven screen, blind, or fence (e.g., of bamboo or reed)
noun, feminine
Synonyms: परदा (pardaa), बाड़ (baad, fence sense)
Frequency: low
Cultural Note: This older sense -- a bamboo or reed screen used to shade a doorway or window, or as a rough fence -- is largely rural and fading from everyday city speech; most Delhi-NCR learners will encounter the excrement sense far more often and may not meet this one at all outside older literature or village contexts.
