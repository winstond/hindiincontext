घाट (ghaat)

noun Primarily a riverbank/landing-stair site (ghaat) -- think of the stone steps leading down to a river or pond, used for bathing, laundry, cremation, or boat landing. A secondary figurative sense appears in the idiom घाट घाट का पानी पीना (ghaat ghaat kaa paanii piinaa, 'to have drunk the water of every ghat' -- i.e. to be worldly-wise from wide experience).
Sense 1 : riverbank steps / landing place
noun, masculine
Synonyms: किनारा (kinaaraa, riverbank generally, not necessarily with steps), तट (tat, literary)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Every Hindi speaker from the Delhi-NCR belt knows Haridwar's Har ki Pauri (har kii paudii) or Varanasi's ghats (ghaat) as the classic image -- stone steps crowded with people bathing, priests doing puja, boats tied up, and sometimes cremation fires burning nearby. The same word covers both a humble village pond's steps and the grand ghats of the Ganga, so scale doesn't change the term.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often translate घाट (ghaat) simply as 'bank' or 'shore,' missing that it specifically implies built steps or a designated landing/bathing/cremation site, not just any riverside spot. 2) Confusing this with घर (ghar, 'house') due to similar sound -- context (river, boats, bathing) should make the distinction clear.

Sense 2 : mountain pass / descent (in place names)
noun, masculine Mostly survives in place names for hill routes, e.g. the Western Ghats (pashchimii ghaat).
Synonyms: दर्रा (darraa, mountain pass, more common word)
Frequency: low
Cultural Note: This sense is why English calls India's two hill ranges the 'Western Ghats' (pashchimii ghaat) and 'Eastern Ghats' (poorvii ghaat) -- घाट (ghaat) here means a mountain slope or pass, not a riverside. A Delhi learner is far more likely to meet the riverbank sense day to day, but this geographic use is worth recognizing.

Sense 3 : worldly experience (idiomatic, in fixed phrase)
noun, masculine Lives almost entirely inside the fixed idiom घाट घाट का पानी पीना (ghaat ghaat kaa paanii piinaa, 'to have drunk the water of every ghat'), meaning someone who is shrewd and worldly-wise from having seen much of life, often with a slightly negative edge (streetwise, hard to fool).
Frequency: low
Cultural Note: You'll hear elders use this idiom approvingly or warily about someone who can't easily be cheated -- a shopkeeper, a politician, an old family friend who has 'seen it all.' It paints worldliness as literally tasting the water of many different ghats (ghaat) along a river's course, each one a different life experience.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes take this idiom literally as being about travel or water rather than about a person's cunning, worldly-wise character. 2) The idiom is almost always used about a third person's nature ('वह घाट घाट का पानी पिया हुआ है' vah ghaat ghaat kaa paanii piyaa huaa hai), rarely in first person, which can feel counterintuitive to learners used to describing themselves.
