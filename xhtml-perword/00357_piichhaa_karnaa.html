पीछा करना (piichhaa karnaa)

verb A compound verb built on पीछा (piichhaa, 'rear/back') with करना (karnaa, 'to do'), meaning to chase, follow, or pursue someone -- physically or, by extension, romantically or persistently.
Sense 1 : to chase, follow (physically)
verb, transitive Takes the person or thing being chased with का (kaa): कुत्ते ने बिल्ली का पीछा किया (kutte ne billii kaa piichhaa kiyaa, 'the dog chased the cat').
Synonyms: पीछे-पीछे जाना (piichhe-piichhe jaanaa, 'to go along behind'), पीछे लगना (piichhe lagnaa, colloquial)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is the standard phrase for a police chase or a dog running after a scooter -- you'll hear it constantly in crime shows and news reports, e.g. पुलिस ने चोर का पीछा किया (pulis ne chor kaa piichhaa kiyaa, 'the police chased the thief').
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often try to mark the object directly with को (ko) as with a normal transitive verb, but पीछा करना (piichhaa karnaa) requires का/की/के (kaa) before the object, since पीछा (piichhaa) itself is a noun being 'done'. 2) Because पीछा (piichhaa) is the grammatical object of करना (karnaa), the verb agrees with पीछा (piichhaa) (masculine) in perfective forms, not with the person being chased -- so it's उसने मेरा पीछा किया (usne meraa piichhaa kiyaa), never की (kii) here.

Sense 2 : to stalk, pursue persistently (often romantically or menacingly)
verb, transitive
Synonyms: पीछे पड़ना (piichhe padnaa, 'to be after someone', colloquial)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This is the word used in Hindi cinema and in real conversation for a boy who won't stop following a girl he likes, and equally for the modern, more serious sense of 'stalking' someone online or in person -- लड़का उसका पीछा कर रहा था (ladkaa uskaa piichhaa kar rahaa thaa, 'the boy was following/stalking her') can mean anything from a filmy crush to a genuine safety concern depending on context.
Common Mistakes: 1) In this sense पीछा करना (piichhaa karnaa) can carry a negative, intrusive connotation (as in stalking) quite different from the neutral physical-chase sense, so tone and context matter -- a learner should not assume it always means an innocent literal chase.
