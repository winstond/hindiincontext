शहीद होना (shahiid honaa)

verb A compound expression built from the borrowed noun/adjective शहीद (shahiid, 'martyr') plus होना (honaa, 'to become'); it means to die a martyr's death, and by extension is used loosely/jokingly for any noble sacrifice or a comic 'wasted' effort.
Sense 1 : to be martyred, to die for a cause
verb, intransitive
Synonyms: बलिदान होना (balidaan honaa, more formal/literary), वीरगति पाना (viiragti paanaa, literary, 'to attain a warrior's death'), जान देना (jaan denaa, broader, 'to give one's life')
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: शहीद (shahiid) comes from Arabic/Persian via Urdu and carries strong emotional weight in Hindi/Urdu media, especially around soldiers, freedom fighters, and Partition-era history; you will see शहीद (shahiid) engraved on war memorials and used respectfully in news reports about soldiers killed in action.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes try to make this transitive by adding ने (ne) marking on the subject -- it stays intransitive, since होना (honaa) governs the construction, not शहीद (shahiid) as an active verb. 2) शहीद (shahiid) itself is invariant here; only होना (honaa) inflects for tense and agreement.
Sample Sentences:
वह सीमा पर लड़ते हुए शहीद हो गया। He was martyred while fighting at the border.
(vah siimaa par ladte hue shahiid ho gayaa।)

Sense 2 : to be sacrificed or wasted (humorous/figurative use)
verb, intransitive Used jokingly about food, money, effort, or objects that get 'sacrificed' in a mundane situation, e.g. a shirt ruined by ink or a plan that falls apart.
Synonyms: बर्बाद होना (barbaad honaa, plainer, 'to be ruined'), कुर्बान होना (kurbaan honaa, similar figurative flavor)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This is classic Hindi banter: friends will say something like 'यह समोसा शहीद हो गया' (yah samosaa shahiid ho gayaa, 'this samosa has been martyred') when someone eats the last one, playfully borrowing the gravity of the literal sense for comic effect. It is very much a spoken-register habit among younger, urban speakers in Delhi-NCR.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners may take this sense too literally and sound overly dramatic in casual contexts where a native speaker intends dry humor -- tone matters more than the words themselves here.
