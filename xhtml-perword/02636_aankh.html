आँख (aankh)

noun Primarily means the physical eye, but extends into a rich set of idioms around perception, attention, and shame/respect.
Sense 1 : the physical organ of sight
noun, feminine
Synonyms: नयन (nayan, literary), नेत्र (netr, literary)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: This is the base literal sense that all the idiomatic uses below grow out of -- once you know आँख (aankh) means 'eye,' phrases like आँख लगना (aankh lagnaa) or आँखें दिखाना (aankhen dikhaanaa) become much easier to remember, since they're just the eye doing something metaphorical.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often default to the singular आँख (aankh) even when talking about both eyes; native speakers usually say आँखें (aankhen) since we naturally have a pair, e.g. मेरी आँखें दुख रही हैं (merii aankhen dukh rahii hain, 'my eyes hurt'), not the singular form.
Sample Sentences:

(merii aankh men kuchh gir gayaa hai।)

Sense 2 : sight, vision, or the faculty of seeing/noticing
noun, feminine Common in phrases like आँखों के सामने (aankhon ke saamne, 'right before one's eyes') and आँख से देखना (aankh se dekhnaa, 'to see with one's own eyes'), where आँख (aankh) stands in for the act or faculty of seeing rather than the organ itself.
Synonyms: नज़र (nazar), दृष्टि (drishti, formal)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: Hindi often frames perception as something that happens to a body part rather than something a person actively does -- notice how आँख (aankh) shows up in dative-subject-flavored expressions of involuntary seeing or noticing, much like दिल (dil) or कान (kaan) do for feeling and hearing.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes confuse आँख (aankh) with नज़र (nazar) -- आँख (aankh) is the physical or perceptual base, while नज़र (nazar) leans toward 'gaze' or 'attention' as a directed action, e.g. उसकी नज़र मुझ पर पड़ी (uskii nazar mujh par padii, 'his gaze fell on me') sounds natural, but the same sentence with आँख (aankh) instead of नज़र (nazar) would sound odd.
Sample Sentences:
मेरी आँख कमज़ोर हो गई है, अब बिना चश्मे के कुछ नहीं दिखता। My eyesight has gotten weak, now I can't see anything without glasses.
(merii aankh kamzor ho gaii hai, ab binaa chashme ke kuchh nahiin dikhtaa।)

Sense 3 : used in idioms about shame, respect, boldness, or emotional restraint
noun, feminine This sense lives almost entirely inside fixed idioms: आँख शर्म करना (aankh sharm karnaa, 'to feel too ashamed to do something'), आँखें दिखाना (aankhen dikhaanaa, 'to intimidate/threaten with a look'), आँखों में धूल झोंकना (aankhon men dhool jhonknaa, 'to deceive someone'), and आँख लगना (aankh lagnaa, 'to fall asleep' or 'to fall in love,' depending on context).
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Family scenes bring these idioms to life: a mother might scold a child with तुम मुझे आँखें दिखाते हो? (tum mujhe aankhen dikhaate ho, 'are you glaring at me?'), while grandparents dozing on a charpai after lunch are the classic image behind आँख लग जाना (aankh lag jaanaa, 'to drift off to sleep'). These expressions are everyday texture, not bookish idioms.
Common Mistakes: 1) These idioms cannot be translated word-for-word -- आँखें दिखाना (aankhen dikhaanaa) does not mean 'to show eyes' in any literal sense, it means to glare threateningly or act tough with someone, often used of a junior being cheeky to an elder. 2) आँख लगना (aankh lagnaa) is ambiguous out of context -- it can mean 'to doze off' (नींद में आँख लगना, niind men aankh lagnaa) or 'to become emotionally attached/fall for someone,' so learners should rely on context to disambiguate.
Sample Sentences:
तुम मुझे आँखें दिखाते हो? Are you glaring at me like that?
(tum mujhe aankhen dikhaate ho?)
