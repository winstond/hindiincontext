डंडा (dandaa)

noun A stick, rod, or staff -- the everyday physical object -- and by extension, a figurative sense of forceful authority or a beating. Also spelled डण्डा (dandaa) with the full-form nasal, though डंडा (dandaa) with the anusvaar is far more common in print and speech.
Sense 1 : stick, rod, staff, cane
noun, masculine
Synonyms: छड़ी (chhadii, thinner, like a walking cane or teacher's cane), लाठी (laathii, stout, associated with watchmen and police), सोंटा (sontaa, colloquial)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Picture a chowkidar (chaukiidaar) doing his rounds at night, thumping his डंडा (dandaa) on the ground as he walks -- that rhythmic sound is a very familiar part of the soundscape in Indian neighborhoods, signaling that someone is on watch.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often reach for छड़ी (chhadii) or लाठी (laathii) interchangeably, but डंडा (dandaa) is the general, all-purpose word -- from a kid's cricket stump substitute to a watchman's night-stick -- while लाठी (laathii) specifically evokes the long bamboo staff carried by chowkidars (chaukiidaar) and police.

Sense 2 : a beating; harsh disciplinary force (figurative)
noun, masculine Common in the fixed phrase डंडे के ज़ोर पर (dande ke zor par, 'by force of the stick', i.e. through coercion) and in डंडा चलाना (dandaa chalaanaa, 'to wield the stick', meaning to use force or discipline someone).
Synonyms: पिटाई (pitaaii, the beating itself, more common for this exact meaning), मार (maar, a hit or blow)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: डंडा (dandaa) shows up constantly in talk about discipline -- a strict school headmaster, a stern father, or an authoritarian boss is often described as running things 'डंडे के ज़ोर पर' (dande ke zor par, 'by force of the stick'), meaning through fear and force rather than persuasion. It captures the same idea as the English 'rule with an iron fist,' but the Hindi image is very literally the stick in hand.
