दाँत (daant)

noun Means 'tooth' in the literal, anatomical sense, and extends into several common idioms built around teeth as a symbol of aggression, greed, or vulnerability.
Sense 1 : tooth (body part)
noun, masculine
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: दाँत (daant) shows up constantly in daily talk -- dentist visits, kids losing baby teeth, or complaining about tooth pain (दाँत में दर्द, daant men dard, 'toothache'). It is also the root of several vivid idioms, covered separately, so this sense stays narrowly the physical tooth.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners often forget the chandrabindu (ँ) and write दात (daat) instead of दाँत (daant) -- the nasalization is essential, not decorative. 2) Some regional/colloquial speech drops the nasal sound, but the written standard keeps it.

Sense 2 : figurative: sharpness, aggression, or a 'bite' (as in ambition or threat)
noun, masculine Occurs almost exclusively inside fixed idiomatic phrases rather than as a freestanding noun with this meaning -- for example दाँत दिखाना (daant dikhaanaa, 'to show one's teeth', i.e., to bare aggression or threaten) or दाँत खट्टे करना (daant khatte karnaa, 'to make someone's teeth go sour', i.e., to thoroughly defeat or humble someone).
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This figurative sense lives almost entirely inside idioms, so a Delhi speaker would rarely say दाँत meaning 'aggression' on its own -- you will hear it packaged in set phrases like दाँत खट्टे कर देना (daant khatte kar denaa) after, say, a cricket team thrashes its rival, or दाँत निकालना (daant nikaalnaa, 'to grin/laugh foolishly') used teasingly among friends.
Common Mistakes: 1) दाँत खट्टे करना (daant khatte karnaa) is not about food or sourness at all -- it means to soundly defeat or teach someone a lesson, so translating it literally will confuse a listener. 2) दाँत दिखाना (daant dikhaanaa) can mean both 'to smile/grin' and 'to bare teeth threateningly' depending on tone and context -- the same phrase carries opposite emotional colors.
