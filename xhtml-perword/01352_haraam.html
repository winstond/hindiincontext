हराम (haraam)

adjective An Arabic-Urdu loanword meaning 'forbidden' (usually in a religious/moral sense), which extends into a strong everyday adjective for something illegitimate or unearned, and lives inside a few very common idioms about wasted effort or corrupted character.
Sense 1 : wasted, spoiled, ruined (of effort, time, or a person's character)
adjective This sense is almost always used with होना (honaa) or करना (karnaa) in fixed phrases about effort, time, or sleep being ruined, rather than as a free-standing descriptive adjective.
Synonyms: बर्बाद (barbaad), ज़ाया (zaayaa, less common)
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: मेरी सारी मेहनत हराम हो गई (merii saarii mehnat haraam ho gaii, 'all my hard work went to waste') is a very common complaint in Delhi households and offices alike -- it frames wasted effort almost as a moral injury, not just an inefficiency. नींद हराम होना (niind haraam honaa, 'to lose sleep, be unable to rest') is the other everyday idiom -- a mother might say her child's exam has made her नींद हराम (niind haraam).
Common Mistakes: 1) मेहनत हराम होना (mehnat haraam honaa, 'effort to go to waste') and नींद हराम होना (niind haraam honaa, 'to lose sleep over something') are the two idioms learners meet most -- don't try to build new sentences with हराम (haraam) meaning 'wasted' outside these set phrases, as it can sound unnatural. 2) It also colors character: हराम का (haraam kaa) or हरामी (haraamii, a related but much cruder word) implies someone is sly or worthless -- know that हरामी (haraamii) is a genuine insult and much stronger than हराम (haraam) itself.
Sample Sentences:

Sense 2 : religiously/morally forbidden
adjective
Synonyms: वर्जित (varjit, formal), नाजायज़ (naajaayaz)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This word is most familiar to Hindi speakers through Urdu and Islamic contexts, most commonly around food -- सूअर का मांस हराम है (sooar kaa maans haraam hai, 'pork is forbidden') is the textbook example every learner in Delhi hears sooner or later. Its opposite is हलाल (halaal, 'permitted'), and the pair is worth learning together.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes reach for हराम (haraam) for any generic prohibition, but it carries a specifically religious/moral weight (as in Islamic dietary law) -- for a simple rule-based ban, मना (manaa) or वर्जित (varjit) is usually more natural. 2) It is invariant -- it does not change form for gender or number, unlike ordinary -आ adjectives.
Sample Sentences:

(islaam men sooar kaa maans haraam hai।)

Sense 3 : illegitimate, unearned, or ill-gotten (extended/figurative use)
adjective
Synonyms: नाजायज़ (naajaayaz), अनुचित (anuchit, formal)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This is the sense behind the common scolding phrase हराम की कमाई (haraam kii kamaaii, 'ill-gotten earnings') and हराम का पैसा (haraam kaa paisaa, 'dirty money') -- money or gain seen as tainted because it was not honestly earned. Parents and elders in North India reach for this phrase a lot when warning kids off shortcuts, bribes, or cheating.
Common Mistakes: 1) In this extended sense हराम (haraam) often appears with का (kaa) -- हराम का पैसा (haraam kaa paisaa, 'ill-gotten money') -- learners sometimes drop the का (kaa) and just say हराम पैसा (haraam paisaa), which sounds off; the possessive link is idiomatic here.
