हवाई (havaaii)

adjective An adjective derived from हवा (havaa, 'air/wind'), used mainly in the literal sense of 'aerial/air-related' and in a common figurative sense meaning 'baseless, imaginary.'
Sense 1 : aerial, of/relating to air travel
adjective
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: You will meet this word constantly in travel contexts -- हवाई जहाज़ (havaaii jahaaz, 'airplane') and हवाई अड्डा (havaaii addaa, 'airport') are everyday vocabulary, not technical terms, since air travel is common across India now.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes try to use हवाई (havaaii) as a noun meaning 'air,' but it is only the adjective form -- the noun is हवा (havaa). 2) It appears fixed in common compounds like हवाई जहाज़ (havaaii jahaaz, 'airplane') and हवाई अड्डा (havaaii addaa, 'airport') -- learners should learn these as set phrases rather than building them freshly each time.

Sense 2 : baseless, imaginary, unfounded
adjective Most often heard in the fixed phrase हवाई बातें (havaaii baaten, 'empty/baseless talk') or हवाई किले (havaaii kile, 'castles in the air').
Synonyms: काल्पनिक (kaalpnik), निराधार (niraadhaar, formal)
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: This figurative sense literally means 'of the air' -- something with no ground beneath it, hence 'baseless.' You'll hear parents or teachers scold with हवाई बातें मत करो (havaaii baaten mat karo, 'do not talk nonsense/baseless things'), and हवाई किले बनाना (havaaii kile banaanaa, 'to build castles in the air') is a lovely shared idiom with English, describing unrealistic daydreaming.
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners may miss that हवाई किले (havaaii kile) is Hindi's version of the English idiom 'castles in the air' -- the imagery is shared but should be recognized as an idiom, not built compositionally. 2) This figurative sense is easy to overlook since the literal 'aerial' sense is far more common in daily speech.

(tum hameshaa havaaii baaten karte ho, koii thos baat nahiin karte।)
