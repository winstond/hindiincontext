कैंची (kainchii)

noun Mainly a concrete noun meaning 'scissors', with a common extended sense describing a scissor-like crossing or straddling motion (e.g., in traffic or acrobatics).
Sense 1 : scissors
noun, feminine
Frequency: high
Cultural Note: Every tailor's shop and beauty parlour in Delhi has a कैंची (kainchii) hanging within reach, and you'll hear shopkeepers casually ask बड़ी कैंची लाओ (badii kainchii laao, 'bring the big scissors') when trimming or cutting cloth. It's an everyday household object right alongside सुई (suii, 'needle') and धागा (dhaagaa, 'thread').
Common Mistakes: 1) Learners sometimes treat this as plural in English fashion ('the scissors are') and get confused about agreement -- कैंची (kainchii) is a regular singular feminine noun in Hindi, so it takes singular feminine agreement: कैंची टूट गई (kainchii toot gaii, 'the scissors broke'), not a plural verb form. 2) Do not confuse with चाकू (chaakoo, 'knife') or ब्लेड (bled, 'blade') -- कैंची (kainchii) specifically means the two-bladed cutting tool.

Sense 2 : a scissor-like crossing or straddling motion
noun, feminine Used attributively or in fixed expressions like कैंची चलना (kainchii chalnaa) or कैंची मारना (kainchii maarnaa) to describe vehicles cutting across each other's path, or legs/limbs crossing in a scissor-like action (as in exercise or dance).
Frequency: medium
Cultural Note: You'll hear this in traffic contexts in Delhi -- a scooter driver weaving sharply between cars might be described as कैंची मार के निकल गया (kainchii maar ke nikal gayaa, 'he cut across and got away'), painting the motion as two blades crossing. It's a vivid, visual extension of the literal tool.
Common Mistakes: 1) This sense is often used specifically for two-wheelers or vehicles weaving dangerously through traffic -- कैंची चलाना (kainchii chalaanaa, 'to cut across/weave, lit. to run the scissors') -- learners may not realize this is idiomatic and try to translate it too literally.
